# Chapter 7: Shared Fire

Lyra was dragging a stone up the slope on her own, and Kael had been watching her do it for four minutes without saying anything, which was a personal record.

It was a squared block of grey granite, the size of a bread oven — one of the old tower facings he had pried loose the day before to use as a stair tread. He had meant to move it with the lever and the rollers.

Lyra had not been told this. Or had been told and had decided it did not apply.

She had a loop of rope around her shoulders and the other end around the block, and she was leaning into the slope at an angle that made his own knee hurt. One slow step at a time, her bad leg trailing behind her like an argument she had lost.

The block moved a hand's breadth. She stopped, breathing hard. She set her feet again.

"Lyra."

"I've got it."

"I can see that you've got it. I can also see your leg."

"My leg is fine."

"Your leg is a different colour from your other leg."

She did not look round. "Then it's my leg. Go and cut your steps."

He put down the saw. It was a small, unfair pleasure to be annoyed, and he did not enjoy it.

He walked across the slope, took the rope off her shoulders before she could shift away, and held it out of her reach like a man taking a toy from a child.

She turned, and for a moment he thought she was going to hit him with something.

"That's four people's work," Kael said. "You've been doing it with one person for years, and it's why you walk like that. It isn't heroic. It's slow. If a thing takes two people, use two people."

"I don't need—"

"You don't get extra points for suffering. I've checked. There's no scoreboard." He lifted the rope. "Take the other end."

She stared at him. The morning was bright and hard, with a cold wind off the cliff, and her face was set in the way of someone deciding whether this was the hill to die on.

Then something went out of her shoulders, and she picked up the rope.

It took the two of them eleven minutes. By the end she was laughing — a short helpless huff that she turned into a cough as soon as she noticed — and the block sat in front of the stair foot with its face squared to the sun.

Kael had not carried a single pound of it that he could have avoided. He thought that was probably the point.

"Don't say anything," Lyra said.

"I'd never."

"You were going to say two people."

"I was going to say it's a very good block."

"Hmph," said Lyra. And, after a moment, to the block: "Two people."

It became a joke by noon. It became something else by dusk.

The stair went up in sections.

He had worked out the first flight the day before, lying awake with a stick of charcoal and the inside of his arm for a notebook, and the numbers had come out cleaner than he liked.

The old stair had been cut to a proportion he recognised: a rise and a run that put a body at ease on a steep climb. The kind a person could take in the dark with a full load of wood.

It was not a stair for soldiers. It was a stair for someone carrying something.

He had a growing feeling about the people who had built this place, and it was not what he'd expected. They did not seem to have been fighters. They seemed to have been people who made a long hard life a little more bearable, with each small kindness set deep into the stone.

They worked through the morning and into the afternoon. He cut and fitted the treads. She planed the edges with a drawknife and a patience that made his own look sloppy, and the stair grew from the foot of the tower in slow stubborn steps.

Around midday a skein of swallows went over low, heading east. A few minutes later another, and another.

"They're early," Lyra said, not looking up.

"Early for what?"

"They leave when the weather's coming. They don't leave this soon." She sighted down a tread, found it fractionally off, and rapped it with her knuckles. "A storm. Not today. Maybe tomorrow, maybe the day after. They come in a day here. You'll see nothing, and then you'll see everything."

He glanced at the sky. It was blue and hard and flawless.

"That's inconvenient."

"It's the Disaster Realm," Lyra said. "It wasn't named after the gardens."

He cleared the last of the rubble from the stair's foot a little before dusk, when the light had gone amber and the shadows were long on the cliff.

It was a heap of broken facing, mostly, wedged against the base of the tower where the old flight had collapsed. He took it down block by block, rolling the big ones out of the way and sorting the small ones into a pile for fill.

The last stone came away with a scrape and a puff of grey dust.

Under it, set flush into the base of the tower wall, was a slab.

Flat and pale, about the width of two hands, the top face carved. He crouched and brushed the dust away with his sleeve.

The marks were small and tidy and ran in two columns, and he knew them before he had thought about it. The same marks as the milestone on the road, four days up the valley. The same neat cuts, the same strange regularity — as though the carver had been writing in a language made to be read by a lot of different people at once.

He put his hand on the slab.

It was warm.

Not hot. Not a warmth like a fire's. A warmth like the inside of a pocket.

He took his hand away and looked at it, then put it back. The stone was the same temperature as before.

The sun had not touched this part of the wall all day. It lay in the deepest shade at the foot of the tower. It was a cold afternoon, with a wind that went through his coat.

Stone did not stay warm like that in the shade.

"Kael?" Lyra called from the yard. "Are you coming up, or are you going to stand there staring at the wall?"

He took off his coat and laid it over the slab, as casually as he could, and went to help her with the wood.

The gate closed behind them as they went up.

He had forgotten that it would. He heard the slow groan of the pin and the long soft knock of the leaf coming home, and turned to see it swing shut on its own behind them — the way it had been built to — and Lyra gave him a look that said *I told you* without a single word.

They climbed the new stair together, each with an armful of split ash. The lower flight was solid under their boots; the upper was still a rope ladder knotted to a stub of stone.

Kael went first. Lyra went after him, and neither of them mentioned that she was never more than a step behind.

At the top, she did not fall.

They fed the beacon together too.

It was a clumsy, domestic business. He built up the fire; she corrected the way he stacked it; they argued mildly about airflow. In the end the flames stood up straight and bright — a yellow tongue against the evening — and he sat on the rim of the pit with his back against the stone and his hands out to the heat.

Lyra did not sit. She stood at the lip of the tower, looking south down the valley at the long line of dark stumps, each with its small square of last light.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and you're not going to say anything about it."

"All right."

"I mean it."

"I said all right."

She breathed in. When she spoke again her voice had flattened, the way it did when she was holding something very heavy and trying to carry it steadily.

"My mother kept this fire. When I was small. She climbed up every night, whatever the weather. I asked her once who it was for. She said it was for the ones who hadn't come home yet."

The fire cracked. Kael did not look up.

"Then one night she went out, and she didn't come back."

Lyra's hand rose to the notch on her ear, and dropped again.

"I lit it that night. I've lit it every night since. I don't know who it's for. I only know she thought someone would see it."

She stopped. It was clear that this was all of it, or all that she could manage.

He thought for a moment about the fine, sharp questions he could ask — where, and when, and how long — and let them all go quietly unasked.

"Thank you," he said.

"I said you weren't going to say anything."

"That isn't anything. That's two words."

A short, startled noise escaped her. Then she sat down on the other side of the pit, her bad leg out in front of her, and the fire between them.

For a while neither said anything. The valley went blue, then grey. Down the line, a long way off, a night bird started up and stopped.

"There's something I'm not telling you," Kael said.

She did not look surprised. "I know."

"It's not that I don't trust you. It's that I don't understand it yet, and if I say it out loud it'll sound like I'm mad."

"Good," Lyra said. "Neither am I."

He looked at her over the fire. She looked back, and the flames moved on her face, and it occurred to him that this was the strangest oath he had ever sworn.

No hands. No promises. Two people on a broken tower, each agreeing to carry their own silence until the other was ready.

*Except we don't, do we,* he thought. *Not alone.*

"Two people," he said.

"Two people," she agreed, with an enormous reluctance that made him grin. "And before you ask, no, you can't put that on the gate."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

"I'm always thinking about it," Kael said. "It's a good gate."

He went down alone, because she was asleep sitting up against the beacon stone with her mouth slightly open, and he could not bring himself to wake her.

He banked the fire. He pulled the rope ladder after him out of habit, and stood at the foot of the new stair in the last blue of the evening.

The wind had changed.

It had been coming from the east all day, cold and dry. Now it came from the north, and there was a taste in it — wet iron and old stone, like a cellar opening.

Out on the slope the snare cords stirred in a way they had not before. Somewhere up the valley, very far off, thunder cleared its throat.

He should have gone straight to bed.

Instead he went to the foot of the tower and lifted his coat off the slab.

It was still warm.

Four hours in the shade, in a rising wind, and it had not cooled by a degree.

He did not take his coat from where it lay. He stood with his hand flat on the slab and found that he was thinking of the crystal in the Heartstone chamber, and a gate standing open in a city he had never seen, and the way his chest had turned over at it — like the last stair of a building he had lived in for years.

The first drop of rain landed on the back of his hand. Then another.

He laid the coat back over the slab, the way you tuck in something sleeping, and went to find a place for the night.
