# Chapter 5: The Open Gate

The first thing Kael learned about the gate was that it had never been hung on straps.

He worked that out lying on his stomach in the dirt of the entrance, sleeve rolled to the elbow, arm in a hole up to the shoulder. The rusted bands on the post were the obvious clue, so obvious he had spent the first hour trusting them. They were thick, riveted, handsome — and they were window dressing. They held the planks together. They did not carry the weight.

A leaf of three-inch oak, taller than a man, would have torn them out of the post in a week.

"What are you doing?" Lyra said.

"Looking for the floor."

"You're standing on the floor."

"The real one." He dug his fingers into the packed silt and felt the grit shift. Under it his knuckles met something cold and smooth and flat. "There."

It took the two of them most of an hour to clear it. Lyra worked without comment, stiff left leg stuck out sideways, a short pick she had produced from nowhere. For the first twenty minutes she said nothing at all. Then, in the tone of a woman picking an argument because it was the only way to speak:

"If it fails tomorrow, this is all wasted."

"It might."

"Then why bother?"

He sat back on his heels and looked at her. Her face was smeared grey to the cheekbones. A strand of ash-pale hair had come loose and stuck to her jaw.

She was not being cruel. She had asked the question of a person who had stopped counting how many things she had mended only to watch them break.

"Because we'll be using it tomorrow," Kael said.

She opened her mouth, and shut it again.

"That's the whole reason," he said. "Somebody might need it. If it only works the day after, we'll be somebody else's problem. Come on. Help me lift."

The stone came up at last in a spray of black silt: a slab the size of a dinner table, set flush with the threshold, a round socket cut into its upper face. The socket was polished. Decades of weight had turned it as smooth as the inside of a bowl, and at its centre, so worn he almost missed it, a dull dark ring where an iron pin had once seated.

Not a hinge, then. A pivot. The leaf had stood on this stone and turned.

He looked up at the post. Then at the lintel, where an answering socket must have been cut in the stone overhead.

And a little cold stir went through him, because the numbers did not work.

The socket was off-centre.

Not by much. Two fingers, perhaps, toward the inside of the yard. But it was the wrong way. A leaf hung on a pivot placed that far from the heel would bind in the frame, grind on its own weight, jam at the first quarter-turn. Any apprentice would have called it a mistake.

He would have called it a mistake himself, an hour ago, with a pencil behind his ear.

And yet the post had been cut for it. The sockets were deep and exact. The chamfers on the frame were shaped to clear a leaf swinging from precisely this point.

Whoever built it had put the pivot two fingers off true, on purpose, and then cut an entire gateway to match.

Why would you do that?

He stood in the dusk with his hand on the cold slab and tried to think it through. A leaf that bound would be useless. A leaf that swung true would sit wherever you left it.

But a leaf with the pivot a little off-line would—

He put his hand on the post, and the answer was there. Not as an idea. As a fact.

His hand knew where the weight would fall. What it would do after. Which cut in the lintel was a gutter and which was a stop.

He had gone to the heap of rotten planking and was already turning a salvaged beam to read its grain before he noticed he had decided to.

It would close. That was the design.

The leaf was hung so that when you let go of it, it drifted shut by itself — slowly — and came home against the stop with its own weight, and locked.

A gate that closes behind you, he thought. Because the person coming through might be carrying something.

He stood there a moment with his hand on the beam. It was an uncomfortable thing, to know something and not know how. Like finding a word in his mouth in a language he had never learned.

It did not feel like a power. It felt like a memory of this place, one he had no business having.

"Kael," Lyra said. "Your hands are shaking."

"It's cold," he said, which was true.

The leaf took the rest of the afternoon.

They pulled the roof timbers from the fallen long house — the biggest oak beams, black with age, sound at the heart — and cut them down with a saw Lyra produced from a cache under the tarpaulin. She had not mentioned the saw. It was, he noticed, a good saw. He did not mention that he had noticed.

He taught her to cut a mortise with the knife and a stone. She taught him a way of lashing a cross-brace she said her father had used, and he learned it in a single try, because it was better than anything he knew.

For a while they worked in a silence with the shape of a truce, passing the heavy end of a plank back and forth without a word, and sometimes their hands met on the same board and both let go and both grabbed again.

At the fourth plank she said, from inside a cloud of sawdust, "My mother always said a bad gate is worse than none."

He did not answer. He suspected that if he said what happened to her? the saw would stop and the afternoon would end.

"Because it tells people to trust it," Lyra said, and drove the blade through the grain.

By the time the sun touched the cliff edge they had a leaf.

It was ugly. The joints did not match. The old straps had been cut and rebent around the edges to hold it together, rust and all, and the whole thing weighed about as much as a small horse. But it was solid, three planks thick, and when he laid it flat and knocked on it with his knuckles it gave back a note like a bell.

They stood over it for a moment, both breathing hard.

"It won't hold," she said.

"It will."

"You can't know that."

"I can't," Kael agreed. And then, because it was the truth and she had earned it: "I just have a feeling about this one."

She gave him a long, flat look. It was the first look she had given him with no suspicion in it. Only tired, and measuring, and slightly stunned.

Then the wind turned and brought the smell down the slope: something dry and animal, like old rope and wet dog. Behind it, from a long way off, a low unhurried sound he had been hearing in his sleep for two nights.

Lyra went to the wall. She stood very still, looking down the valley. When she came back, her face was a different shape.

"They're early," she said.

They raised the leaf with a lever and a stack of cribbing, the sun going down at their backs and the first grey shapes already visible at the foot of the slope. Forty of them. Perhaps more.

They were not rushing. They came up the funnel in a long, patient river, and where the pits and cords caught them there was a brief ugly scuffle, a yelp, a stop, and the others stepped around the fallen and kept coming.

Lyra went up to the wall with her bow. He heard the string sing — once, twice, a steady count like a clock — and below the wall a hound went down with an arrow in the neck.

He did not look. He fitted the pin.

A length of oak, thick as his forearm, shod with the broken hinge-stubs. He seated it in the socket, took the weight of the leaf on the lever, and said, to nobody, "Please," which he had not planned to say.

The leaf went up. It teetered.

For one long second all its weight rested on the pin, and the pin rested on the stone, and the stone rested on whatever was under the stone, and the whole thing hung there in the last of the light, balanced on the judgement of somebody who had died a long time ago.

He let go.

It swung. Slowly. Not toward the wall, as he had half feared, and not out into the dark. It swung toward the open frame, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice, and came round with its own weight — gathering speed, a heavy, certain, unhurried arc.

A hound reached the threshold as it came. Kael saw the long jaw, the pale smear where eyes should be, the head lifting.

The gate struck home.

It made a sound like the end of an argument. The stop took it. The lintel took it. The pin turned once in the socket with a long low groan and was still, and the whole frame shook, and dust came down in a fine grey sheet from the stone overhead.

On the other side, something hit the planks with all its weight, and bounced, and hit them again.

The gate did not move.

They did not sleep that night either.

They sat on the inside of the yard with their backs against the new leaf, shoulder to shoulder because it was cold, and felt the hounds throw themselves at it in rhythm. A dull, patient impact, like a heartbeat in the wood. Every few minutes the planks shuddered. Every time, the pin groaned in its socket, and the leaf did not give an inch.

"It's holding," Lyra said, to herself, as though reading it off a gauge.

"It's holding."

"I've watched that gap for years. Years, and I never thought..." She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. When she took it away she was looking at the lintel.

She laughed, once, short and cracked, as if the sound had been in storage. "Look at me. Talking to a gate."

"It's listening," Kael said. "It's very well made."

"Don't." But the corner of her mouth had moved, and stayed moved for a full breath before she put it away.

Kael had wiped the lintel down at dusk, to find the stop. Grime had come away on his sleeve.

And under the grime, on the stone above the socket, cut deep and even, there was a mark.

A star. Seven points.

He had seen it before, on a milestone, by a road. He did not say so.

Beside him, something in Lyra's body had gone entirely still.

"Where did you learn that?" she said.

"I didn't. It was under the dirt."

"Don't," she said, very quietly. "Don't lie to me about this one."

"I'm not lying." He turned his head. "Lyra. I swear."

She stared at the star for a long time. The hounds hit the gate again, and the planks shook, and she did not seem to hear it.

When she spoke, it came out small and slow, as if she were laying each word on a table to see if it would hold.

"There's one on the underside of the beacon stone," she said. "I found it when I was twelve. I've kept the fire lit over it every night since."

Kael did not say anything. There was nothing in him that would have been the right thing to say.

He sat with his back against the oak and let her have the silence, and behind them the gate took another blow, and held.
