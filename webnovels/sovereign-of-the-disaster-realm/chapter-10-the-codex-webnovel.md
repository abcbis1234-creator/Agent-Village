# Chapter 10: The Codex

The stone was hot enough to hurt.

Kael knelt in the wet yard with his wrapped hands hanging useless at his sides and watched the ledger slab breathe. That was the only word for it. The dull pulse came up through the hollow at even intervals, slow as a resting man's heart, and with every pulse a hairline of pale light ran out along the carved channels and faded again.

He had seen light in stone exactly once before in his life — the phosphor they painted on the inside of a mine shaft — and this was not that. That had been chemistry. This felt like being looked at.

"Lyra," he said.

She did not answer. She was still kneeling at the beacon pit with her back to him and her hand in the ash.

Kael put his palm flat on the stone.

The light under his hand changed.

It went from pale to white, and then — for a moment that made no sense in any language he had — it went *in*. Not into the stone. Into him. There was no pain. It was like drinking cold water through the skin of his hand.

And then the world opened a window.

It was not in front of his eyes. It was in the same place his thinking happened, a bright flat rectangle of nothing-grey with lines of script in it that he could read even though he had never learned the script, and he understood as it appeared that the knowledge was not arriving as letters but as meaning, and the letters were only how his mind had chosen to lay the meaning out.

**Sovereign Candidate: Unverified.**
**Calibration: 0%.**
**Status: CONFIRMED.**

He stopped breathing.

**Custody locus detected: Watchpost Nine, Old Road Marches.**
**Keeper: none. Vacant: 9 cycles.**
**Structural integrity: 14%.**

Kael read that number twice.

**Authority: present.**
**Mana Capacity: none.**
**Mana Core: ABSENT.**

And under it, small, as though it were a footnote to his own life:

**Mana Core restoration: not possible. Mana Core free-form restoration: initiated. Base: Authority.**

Something in him moved.

It was not a wound and it was not a healing. It was like a knot he had carried so long he had forgotten it was a knot being worked loose from the inside; a slow, hot, grinding give. His hands clenched on the wet stone. The grey yard swam.

Then the pressure stopped, and the window changed.

**Mana Core: FUNCTIONAL.**
**Capacity: 1.**
**Warning: capacity outside standard calibration. Field unstable. Recalibration required at threshold.**

One.

He had heard the number Leon's Heartstone had read, shouted across a courtyard full of people. Eight thousand four hundred. Hero-Class. The sound the crowd made had been like the sea.

One was not a class. One was not even a bad joke. One was what a stone might show if you put it under the tongue of a dead man and were generous.

And yet his fingers were tingling, all the way to the tips, in a way they had not tingled since he was fifteen. And his ribs — he had cracked something, he was sure of it now, the flat crack in the dark —

**Physical injury: outside keeper scope.**
**Recommendation: rest.**

"Wonderful," Kael said.

**Authority Sight: AVAILABLE.**

He flinched. The window did not care.

**Use of Authority Sight consumes calibration. Current reserve permits a limited reading.**

He did not know what that meant, and he wanted it anyway, the way he had wanted to take apart his first bicycle when he was six and had no idea how to put it back.

He said, out loud, because saying it out loud seemed to be the whole of it: "Yes."

The world became a diagram.

That was the only way he could describe it afterward, and Lyra would ask him to describe it twice. The yard did not change into anything. Nothing glowed. Nothing sprouted arrows. But everything he looked at arrived with a second thing laid over it, the way a shape arrives with its reflection when you set it on still water.

The gate, in the corner of his eye, was oak and iron and wet black wood. And it was also a line, and the line had a bend in it, and the bend was two fingers off the true across forty feet of span — a curve he had found for himself with a chalk string and a level, and that the diagram showed him at a glance, in a way that felt like being told something he had always known.

The tower was forty feet of torn masonry and a bowed beam. And it was also a shape with a grey line through the middle of it — a line that ran from the bell of the foundation up through the wall and out of the top, a line drawn through the place the tower had always wanted to be. And along that line, glowing like coals in a cold hearth, were eleven small knots of bright.

Eleven.

*Eleven places where it was mended,* Kael thought. *Eleven hands that did the work.*

And the beam. The beam was the worst of it — a timber as thick as his body, bowed now, its underside opened by the wind. The diagram put a shape over that crack, and the shape was not the beam. It was a joint. Two matched pieces of wood cut to wedge into each other and lock, with a scarf cut and a key driven through, and the shape sat over the crack like a hand fitted to a wrist.

He had repaired a cattle shed like that once. He had not known he still remembered it.

Then the vision went dim at the edges and the window came back.

**Reserve depleted. Authority Sight: unavailable.**

The diagram drained away and left him with the yard, dark and wet and full of splintered planks, and with the great crack in the beam that he could no longer see.

Kael sat down hard in the mud.

He did not get up for a while.

---

The Cache opened at the base of the beacon.

He had looked at that foundation a hundred times. It was the oldest stone in the yard, six courses of it, the courses irregular and set with lime. He had thought the seam at the bottom corner was a fault in the rock. The diagram had given him a number: *Founder's Cache. Sealed. Key: keeper.*

"Aren't I provisional?" he said, to the window.

**Keeper Status: PROVISIONAL.**
**Cache access: granted. Custody: pending acceptance.**

He put his hands on the seam and pushed, and the stone slid in — not out, in — and made a sound like a lock he had never heard, and a small square doorway opened in the foundation. The interior was dry as a cupboard. Somebody had built it dry.

Inside were four things.

A knife in a hide sheath, the handle worn smooth in two places, the way a handle wears where a hand always takes it.

A mason's hammer, the head blued with age, the haft shortened and re-fitted, clearly more than once.

A coil of cord — thin, about two fingers thick, wrapped in waxed cloth. He picked it up and found it absurdly light, and something about the way it lay in his fist made his skin prickle.

And a book.

He had expected a book, and he had not known that he had, and when his hands closed on it he felt a small, ridiculous, unscientific shock — that in this world of zero-mana villages and broken watchposts and hounds, somebody had made a book.

It was thin. The cover was a slab of hide stretched tight and sewn at the spine with sinew. No title, no ornaments. The pages were vellum, and dry, and when he opened it the smell that came up out of it was the smell of a room that had been shut a long time.

The first page was a drawing of the gate.

Not a plan. A drawing: three-quarters view, done in ink with a sure hand — the same gate that stood in the yard, but whole, with both leaves hung and the pivot stone visible and the stop cut clean. Under it, a curving line, and a row of small notches along the line.

He turned the page.

The second page was the star.

Seven points, the same seven-pointed star that had been in the gate's ironwork, that had been under the grime at the gate's centre. Longer at the bottom. This one was inked in careful segments, and beside each point was a column of small marks he could not read.

He turned the page.

A map.

He knew it was a map. The valley was a shape he recognised — the funnel below the tower, the wall, the shoulder of the ridge — and the drawing had his tower on its spur, and from the tower a fine line ran away south along the ridge and was marked at intervals with small stars.

Not five. He could see five stars before the line went over the shoulder of the hill, and beyond the shoulder there were more marks — many more, thinning with perspective, going away into the drawn distance of the page.

The chain did not end at five.

The last pages of the book were blank.

He turned to the flyleaf. There, in the front of a book whose pages were otherwise all drawing, somebody had written.

He could not read it. The hand was old, and cursive, and the ink had gone brown. But the window, which was patient, laid itself over the page and gave him the meaning of five words in the top-left corner of the writing:

**First Record: available.**
**Subject: Watchpost Nine.**
**Offer: custody.**

And beneath it, in the book's own brown ink, in a hand that shook on the last stroke:

*The work is not to be finished in one lifetime. This is the design, and it is not a cruelty.*

He sat in the mud in the cold morning with the book open on his knees and read that line over and over.

Nine cycles. Nine cycles of a ledger with nobody to write in it, and one entry at the front, written by somebody who knew they would never see it finished and had decided to be glad.

**Do you accept custody?**

"They'll ask me to rule," he said, out loud, to the window.

The window said nothing. It had nothing to say. That was not what it had asked.

**Do you accept custody?**

He thought about a boy in a courtyard with a heart made of nothing. He thought about a wall being built and repaired and repaired, and about a gate hung to close itself, and about a stair somebody had pegged. Nothing in it was ownership. Nothing in it was a crown. It was work, and it did not end, and the only thing being asked of him was that he not put it down.

"No one carries it alone," he said.

**Custody accepted.**
**Keeper: Kael Arden. Provisional.**
**Watchpost Nine: Assigned.**
**Current integrity: 14%.**

He closed the book and put both hands flat on the ledger stone, and the stone was warm again, and steady, and quiet.

---

Lyra came down at mid-morning.

She came down the rope ladder, not the stair, hand over hand, and it took her a long time on the last few rungs because the ladder ended four feet above the rubble and she had to drop — and she landed badly, on the left leg, and stood there with her jaw set until the pain passed.

She had ash on her forehead where she had pushed her hair back with a dirty hand.

"I can't light it," she said. "There's nothing in the pit worth lighting. The coal's wet."

"Then don't light it today."

"Don't." She looked at him. "Do you hear yourself?"

"I hear myself." He held up the book. "Lyra. I need to show you something, and I need you to sit down."

She did not sit down. But she looked at the book, and she went very still.

She knew what it was. He could see her know it, the way a person knows a face before they know the name.

"Where did you find that?"

"Under the beacon."

She took two steps and stopped. Two steps was all. Then she folded down onto the well curb, quite suddenly, as though the strength had gone out of her legs, and sat with her hands in her lap, and did not reach for it.

"Nobody could find it," she said. "My mother looked. My grandmother looked. It's written in the old stories — that the founders left a book in the stone and that when the keeper came back it would open."

"I'm sorry it opened for me."

"Don't be sorry." Her voice was thin and hard. "Tell me what it says."

He told her. He told her the number for integrity, in brackets, as a number and not a metaphor, because she would want to be told that way. He told her about the vision — how it made the tower into a shape, and made eleven knots along the middle of it, and made the broken beam into a joint. He told her about the map, and stopped.

"What?"

"Let's get the beam fixed first."

"Kael."

"First the beam," he said. "Because if the beam goes, none of it matters."

She looked at him with an expression he had not seen on her before — not suspicion, which he would have understood. Something quieter, and older. Something like being asked to hold on to a rope you could not see the end of.

Then she said, "You've done something."

"I've accepted a job."

"A job." She laughed. It was a short, ugly, startled sound. "You're provisional. That's what you said. Keeper, provisional—" She stopped. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "So it's true. You're the man in the stories."

"The stories have it wrong." He picked up the founder's hammer. It was lighter than the one he'd been using and it sat in his hand as though it had been waiting. "There isn't a man in the stories. There's a post, and a chain, and it's been nine cycles of nobody."

"Nine cycles."

"Nine cycles."

She sat on the well curb with her hands in her lap and stared past him at the black beacon pit.

Then she said, "So you can fix my stair."

"The stair's easier than the beam."

"The stair first," Lyra said. "In the morning. If I get that ladder right, I can walk up to my own fire." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and completely steady. "And Kael — if you're going to do this, you do it right. You don't half-build a stair and leave it to the wind, and then say it's fine."

"I know."

"You say that a lot."

"It keeps being true."

She got up. She went past him to the tower wall, where the ax was stacked, and she picked it up, and stood there.

"You said the beam picks up the bell of the foundation," she said. "Then we'd better know what we're building before we climb. Get your book."

He got his book.

He opened it on the well curb, and she stood over it with the ax on her shoulder, and together they spent the last hour of that morning working out an honest answer to the word *tomorrow*.

Where the rope ladder went, they marked with charcoal. The board he would first cut they marked with an X.

And when they had done with the map, he turned one page back, and they looked at the star.

The seven points. Each with its small column of marks he could not read.

"Seven," Lyra said. Then, after a moment, in a voice that had gone very careful: "Kael. Look at the map again."

He looked.

The fine line ran away south along the ridge. Five stars were in sight. Past the shoulder of the hill there were marks, more and more of them, thinning into the drawn distance.

And along the southern edge of the paper, where the map gave up and the ink stopped, the line was still going.

She put her finger on the place where it stopped. Her nail was black with ash.

"How far does it go?" she said.

"I don't know," Kael said. "The book does not know either. He stopped drawing."

The wind came down off the ridge, and the unlit brazier at the top of the tower was cold, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Lyra brushed the ash off her finger and said, "First the beam. Then we find out."
