# Chapter 8: First Fall

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and the sky it left behind looked wrong.

Kael noticed it first as a lack. No wind in the yard. No birdsong. Nothing moving on the slope but a few strands of mist lying flat in the funnel like spilled milk. The light came down yellow and thick, the colour of an old bruise.

He stood at the foot of the stair with his coat in one hand and a lump of cold, wet bread in the other, and told himself that the weather in a strange world was none of his business.

Then he lifted the coat off the slab, and it was still warm.

He had known it would be. That was what bothered him. Overnight he had half-persuaded himself that he'd imagined it — that exhaustion and a bad week had made a pocket of heat out of a patch of sun. But the stone sat there in its hollow with the rain beaded on its face and a faint ghost of vapour rising from the cuts, and it was warmer than his hand.

"You're up early," Lyra said.

She was coming down the new stair one careful step at a time, the left leg stiff, her bow slung over her back. The beacon had been tended; he could see the smoke.

He had told himself he would say nothing until she'd eaten. He found he had no talent for it.

"There's something I want to show you."

She stopped on the bottom step. She looked at the slab, then at his face, and her own took on the expression of someone being asked to hold an ember.

"Is this the thing you're not telling me?"

"Part of it. The part I can show."

"All right," Lyra said, and came down.

She crouched beside the stone and looked at it for a long time without touching it. Then she reached out and laid two fingers on the top face, where the marks ran in their two neat columns.

"Huh," she said.

That was all. She took her hand away, wiped it on her knee, and went on studying the carving with her head tilted a little to one side, like a woman checking a joint for rot.

Kael waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. It took him a moment to understand that she had felt the cold, and had felt him watching her feel it, and had decided, for reasons of her own, to say nothing at all.

He decided not to say anything either. He put his hand beside hers on the slab, and it was warm.

"It's a list," Lyra said, after a while.

"It's a log."

"What's the difference?"

"A list is what you have. A log is what you did." He traced the left-hand column with the tip of the screwdriver, not quite touching. "That side is the dates, or something like them. See how the marks cluster? Three, then five, then seven, and back down? That's a cycle. A calendar, probably, of something that repeats."

"And the other side?"

"That's the work." He tapped the air above the right-hand column. The pictograms were small and tidy, a dozen different shapes repeated in careful rotation. A wavy line with a hook at the bottom. A rectangle with a bar across it. A circle with a dot. "Water. Wall. Hinge, I think. I've seen this layout a hundred times. Every building I've ever worked in had one, taped inside a cupboard door. Gutters, mortar, hinges, drains. The same four jobs, again and again, in a ring."

"Somebody wrote down every time they fixed the tower."

"Every time. Look at the tick marks." There were short strokes after each pictogram, one to a few. "The ones after the wall mark are how many courses they relaid. This one after the hinge—" He stopped. His throat felt odd. "Is the gate."

She stared at him.

"They fixed it," Kael said. "The gate. Over and over. Look — here's an entry, and here's another, and here's another, once in every cycle. Somebody kept hanging that gate, and kept writing it down, and—"

He ran his eye down the long column, past more entries than he could count at a glance.

Hundreds.

The intervals grew wider toward the bottom. The strokes grew rarer, as though the hand holding the chisel had grown tired.

And near the base of the slab, in an empty field of unmarked stone, they simply stopped.

Neither of them said anything.

"How long?" Lyra said.

"I don't know." He cleared his throat. "Long enough to wear a groove in a pivot stone."

It was a stupid thing to feel, so he did not say it. But he looked down at the blank place where the ledger ended, and what he felt was a kind of grief. The specific, ridiculous grief of finding out that someone had kept up a job, faithfully, for generations, and had then simply been interrupted.

Nobody had finished. Nobody had written *this is the last repair*. The chisel had just been put down.

"Kael," Lyra said.

She was looking at the sky.

The cloud was coming up the valley from the north.

It was not the grey, shapeless sheet he had expected. It had an edge. It stood on the horizon like a wall of ink — hard-lined, flat on top, with a bruise-coloured fringe along its lower edge where the rain was already falling in dark curtains.

And it was moving. He watched a single ruined tower, five stumps down the line, disappear into it without a ripple.

"How long?" he said.

"Evening. Maybe sooner." Lyra's voice was very calm. "You said you wanted to understand it before the storm. You've got an hour. Maybe two."

"And the work?"

"The work is the roof, and the gate, and the stores, and the stair, and all the wood, and the water, and the fire." She said it all in one breath. "I can do about half of it alone."

"Then you can't do it alone."

"No," Lyra agreed. "Neither can you."

He looked at the slab. It lay in its hollow with the dull gold of morning on its face, patient as ever. There was more to read, and he could feel it — a whole language set out in tidy rows. Another hour with it and he might understand the cycle. Another two and he might tell her what the circle with the dot meant.

It was the first real answer he had found in this world, and it was warm under his palm.

And behind him, the storm came on.

"We'll finish tomorrow," Kael said.

"You could stay," she said. "I'd manage."

"You'd manage badly." He stood. "It'll still be here tomorrow."

"Will it?"

"That's the point of a ledger," Kael said, and pulled his coat over the slab, and set a flat stone on the hem to hold it against the wind.

They did not talk for the rest of the day, except to say *Left* and *Lift* and *Hold that*.

The roof first. It was the one job they could not afford to lose. They went up on the rafters with rope and with every loose stone the yard could spare, and Lyra worked the eaves while he worked the ridge, and where a board lifted in the rising wind they weighted it and lashed it and weighted it again.

His hands bled in three places. He did not notice until later.

The stores came second: they dragged the dried meat and the water skins and what remained of the bread into the dry north corner — which had been Lyra's idea, and which was already vindicated by the damp stain on the south wall — and sealed the doorway against the draught with a hide.

The gate took ten minutes. He walked the leaf through its whole arc, checked the pin and the socket and the stop, wedged the pivot with a thin oak shim, and stepped back.

It sat in its frame like a patient animal. It did not need him.

"It's fine," he said.

"You keep saying that to it."

"It keeps being true."

He looked down the slope while he said it, out of habit, and the habit turned up nothing. No grey shapes at the edge of the grass. No breathing in the dark. There had been no hounds at all since the night of the gate — not one, not even a stray at the far end of the funnel.

Lyra had said nothing about it for two days. He understood now that this was how she said the things that frightened her.

"It's quiet," he said.

"I noticed." She did not look up from the knot she was tying. "Don't say it again. It makes it worse."

The stair was the problem.

He had built it in two days with half the lumber he wanted, and the new treads were pegged but not braced. Along its outer edge there was nothing to hold the thing to the tower but its own weight and a few iron staples he had salvaged from the old gate.

In a calm evening it would stand for a hundred years. In a gale it was a long wooden ladder leaning on a wall.

He could brace it. He needed rope, which they had used on the roof, and timber, which they had used on the roof, and an hour, which the storm had taken.

"I'll tie it off with what's left," he said.

"There isn't enough."

"There's some."

"Kael." Lyra put her hand flat on the nearest tread. In the darkening yard she looked small and tired and very sure. "It's the stair, or it's the store. You said so yourself. We've done the store. Leave it."

"If it goes—"

"Then it goes." She said it as plainly as she had said anything. "We can build it again. I'd rather build it again than do the roof twice."

It was the first time she had made a decision for both of them. He realised that it was also the right one, and that he had not liked being told.

He took his hand off the tread.

"Two people," he said, with a sourness that surprised them both.

"Two people," Lyra said. "Even when one of them's wrong."

The storm reached the valley at the hour when the light turned the colour of slate.

They were at the top of the tower by then, because she would not leave the fire. He had argued, briefly, and lost, and then climbed after her on the new stair with the wood on his shoulder and his heart going faster than the climb justified.

The wind had risen in the last hour from nothing to a steady, shoving force, and the whole structure hummed with it — a deep low thrum he felt in his teeth.

Lyra lit the fire with her back to the wind and her coat held open like a wing, three tries and a curse before the kindling caught. It rose, small and stubborn, a lick of orange in a blue-black world.

She fed it. She fed it again.

And then she stood up and looked south, and he followed her gaze.

Down the whole length of the valley, the beacons were dark.

All five of them. Stump after stump, along their spurs of rock. Nobody had lit them in longer than either of them could guess. Only the tower's fire answered the dark — small and stubborn and entirely alone — and he found he did not like how little it looked.

Then the wall of cloud reached the tower.

It struck like something solid. The wind hit the fire flat and laid it down, and the flames lay along the stone like hair in a river. Rain came sideways, hard as thrown gravel. The tower shuddered.

He grabbed Lyra's arm and she grabbed his, and below them, in the black, there came a long wrenching groan from the foot of the tower as thirty new treads of oak leaned into the wind.

"It's holding!" Lyra shouted, into his ear.

For a moment, Kael almost believed her.
