# Chapter 11: Restore

Kael woke before light because he was cold, and stayed awake because of his ribs.

He had slept in the storehouse with his back against the north wall, on a bed of cut heather that Lyra had thrown down without comment. His coat was gone — the wind had taken it off the slab two days ago and it was somewhere in the valley, or in a hound's nest, or in the sea — so he had slept in a shirt that still smelled of rain.

Every breath went in like a blade under the arm. He lay still and counted them, and somewhere past fifty he stopped counting and simply lay there and listened to the yard.

Water dripping. The gate's low complaint as it moved an inch in the morning air and settled.

It had not moved on its own before. He lay very still and listened to it again.

The gate had not been touched by wind. There was no wind. It had moved because something had leaned on it.

He got up, which took a while, and went out.

The yard was grey and wet and littered. A torn board hung off the storehouse eave. The rope ladder at the tower's foot lay coiled in the mud where he had left it, and the wreck of the stair was a low dark heap with oak sticking out of it like ribs.

And the gate stood in its frame, quiet, with the morning light coming through the gap under the leaf in a thin gold line.

He walked down and stood in front of it. The stop was still wedged with the flat stone he'd put under the hem. Nothing had been cut. Nothing had been forced.

*It moved because the post settled,* he thought. *Because the stone has finally bedded itself after forty years.*

He stood there and did not believe it, and the not-believing sat in him like a stone in a boot.

"You're up early again."

Lyra was on the tower steps with the ax in her hand. She had wrapped her left knee with a strip of hide and tied it tight, and she stood with the leg out at an angle.

"I'm going up," Kael said. "Not the platform. Just the underside of the beam."

"The underside of the beam is forty feet up a wall."

"I know."

"You can't climb it with your hands like that and a rib like that."

"I know." He nodded at the heap. "That's not for climbing. That's for working."

She looked at the stair wreckage for a long moment. Then she looked at the tower, and then back at the wreckage, and he watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had.

"How much of it do we need?" she said.

"Half. Maybe less. I don't need to stand up there. I need to reach the crack from below and cut a joint in two pieces of wood with my arms over my head." He flexed the bandaged hands and hissed. "I need to work slow. I'll need somebody to hold the far end steady."

"Then I'm holding the far end."

"Your knee."

"My knee holds." She started toward the heap. "You said you needed a person. You've got a person. Try not to be clever about it."

They built it in two hours.

It was not a stair. He was very clear about that from the start, and said it three times, and Lyra got tired of hearing it by the second time. It was a working frame: two of the longer rails set as legs against the wall, cross-braced with offcuts, and a single plank platform at the height of the beam's underside. A ladder of the kind they used in the mines went up the near face. No handrail. Nothing decorative. Nobody was ever going to walk up it in a storm and be proud of themselves.

He tested every peg by putting his whole weight on it and then bouncing. Two came out. He drove them back with the founder's hammer, and on the fourth strike the head rang a note he had never heard out of a hammer before — a clean, low note that hung in the air a moment too long.

Lyra looked up at him.

"Did you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"The hammer."

"It's blued," Kael said. "Old steel rings."

"It didn't ring before."

He turned the hammer over in his hand. The head was blue-black, unmarked, worn bright in two places along the striking face. The haft had been re-fitted, and cut short, and re-fitted again.

He could not explain the note. He put it in the same place he had been putting everything else this week, and went back to work.

The crack ran four feet along the beam's underside, and it had opened like a lip. He got up on the platform on his belly, and lay there with his face against cold oak, and looked at it from underneath, and the sight of it made his stomach turn over.

This was not a crack that could be screwed and forgotten. The beam had been loaded past what it was ever meant to carry — the platform's whole weight, plus the beacon, plus a wind the builders had not planned for — and the wood had given along the grain.

Wood does that. It does not break like a stick. It splits along its length, and once it starts it keeps going.

But it had not finished going. The split had arrested halfway along, where the grain turned, and the timber was still one piece. That was the whole of his margin. One piece, with four feet of split in it.

"You're quiet," Lyra said from below.

"I'm reading it."

"Read faster. My knee's telling me about the weather."

He lay there and let his eyes go over the crack, and he was not looking at the split. He was looking at the grain around it. That was the thing his old master had made him do, over and over, until it became a habit he could not break: *don't look at the damage, boy. Look at what's holding.*

And around the split, holding, was a great mass of good oak. Solid, dry, and — he pressed his thumb into it and felt the give — still alive. The beam was not failing. The beam had failed at one place and was being asked to carry the whole thing from there.

Which was, in a way he did not say out loud, the same as the watchpost.

"A scarf," he said.

"A what?"

"A scarf joint." He pushed himself back from the edge and sat up. "I'll cut two matching bevels in the good wood either side of the split, and drop a key through, and peg it. The load goes through the joint instead of through the crack. It's how you'd mend a roof beam in a house. My master made me do it eleven times on a barn before I got it right."

"Eleven times."

"Twelve. The last one was the one he kept."

Lyra was quiet for a second. Then: "Then do it."

He cut for three hours.

The saw was a salvaged thing with two teeth missing and a handle someone had carved by hand. He used it on the offcuts until his palms, under the bandages, were raw, and then he stopped and sat with his back to the tower and breathed shallowly until the shaking went out of his arms.

"You could stop," Lyra said.

"I could."

"I'd not think less of you."

"I know." He looked at the sky, which was high and pale and moving. "I've been the man with zero for a week, Lyra. I've spent a week being useful, and every hour of it I've been waiting for somebody to tell me it doesn't count. It's not that I can't stop. It's that I've finally started, and it turns out I don't want to."

She did not answer that. She sat down on the rubble beside him, which she had not done all week, and put the flat of her hand on the stone for a moment, and took it away.

Then they went back to work.

The bevels came out clean. He cut them by eye and checked them with a straightedge made from a broken tile, and the two faces met with a sound he liked — a soft, solid *thock* that meant the wood had found its partner.

He cut the key last. A tapered wedge, driven down through the joint to draw the two faces hard together. When he set it and struck it once, the whole beam took the load with a groan that went through the tower and up into the platform and came back down again, and the crack under his hand closed by the width of a hair.

"That's it," he said.

"That's it?"

"That's it. It'll never be a new beam. But it won't go on me."

Lyra stood under the platform with her head back, looking up at nothing, because there was nothing to see from there. Then she said, in a completely level voice:

"Say the number."

"Lyra—"

"Say the number."

"Fourteen," Kael said.

"Fourteen what?"

"Fourteen percent. It's what the stone told me. That's the whole watchpost — the wall, the well, the storehouse, the gate, the stair, the platform, all of it. Fourteen percent of what it was built to be."

She took that in. She was not a woman who needed things softened, and she did not soften this one.

"Then it's twice what it was this morning."

"That's the wrong arithmetic."

"Is it?"

He thought about it. He thought about the number the window had given him and the number the beam was giving him, and he found he could not honestly say that she was wrong.

"Get the book," he said.

They took the ledger out to the yard and set it on the well curb in the light.

The slab had cooled to the temperature of the morning. The two columns of marks ran down its face, old and tidy, and at the bottom the blank field where the hand had stopped.

"Show me what to do," Lyra said.

"You don't have to be here for this."

"I carried the water for this. I'm here."

Kael knelt, and took the founder's chisel, and set the point against the stone at the bottom of the right-hand column — in the blank, where the last mark had ended.

He did not know the shape. He had not been told. His hand knew anyway: a small circle, and a dot in the middle of it. Beside it, a single tick.

The chisel bit, and the stone gave, and the sound was the sound of every repair he had ever finished.

He stood up. His ribs screamed and he ignored them.

**Ledger resumed.**
**Keeper: Kael Arden. Provisional.**
**Record: 1.**

Below the new mark, in the stone, a line of light ran along the carved channel and faded.

"That's it?" Lyra said. "That's the whole thing? You scratch a circle and it's a record?"

"It's a record of what was done." He put the chisel down. "Somebody did it before me, and somebody will do it after. The mark isn't for us. It's for whoever's standing here in nine cycles and can't tell whether the wall was ever fixed."

She looked at the two columns, and at the small new mark at the bottom, and he saw her understand it in the same way he had, all at once, without a word.

"My mother kept a tally," Lyra said. "Not in stone. On the doorframe, inside, where the rain couldn't get. Every night she lit the fire she put a scratch. There were four hundred and six."

Kael looked at the blank stone in front of him.

"Then we're behind," he said.

He saw the traveller at noon.

He was up on the platform — the frame held, and he had gone up to check the joint with his hand and to look at the sky — when the shape appeared at the shoulder of the road, far down in the funnel.

He froze. His hand was on the beam.

For one long second he was certain, and the certainty had a taste: iron, and old fear.

But it was not low to the ground. It did not move like the hounds moved, in that awful liquid run. It came up the road at the pace of a person who had been walking a long way, one foot and then the other, with something over one shoulder.

It stopped at the place where the road bent around the last spur, and stood there.

And then it lifted one arm.

"Lyra," Kael said.

She was below him, in the yard, with her hand on the ax.

"I see it."

"It waved."

"I see it." Her voice had gone flat and very careful, and she was standing absolutely still. "Nobody waves."

The figure stood at the bend for a long moment, arm still up, and did not come closer.

"Get down," Lyra said. "Slowly. And bring the hammer."

Kael came down the frame with his heart going. At the bottom she was already moving — not toward the gate, but to the wall, to the loophole beside it where the stone was broken away and you could see the whole road without being seen.

He crouched beside her and looked out.

The traveller had come nearer. Close enough now to see a coat the colour of old leaves, a pack, and a staff — a proper walking staff, notched, with a bundle tied to it.

And close enough to see that the traveller was limping.

"Kael," Lyra said, very quietly, without taking her eyes off the road. "When he gets here. You do the talking."

"Why me?"

"Because I've lived here eleven years and never once seen anybody walk up that road." She drew an arrow from the quiver at her back, slowly, and set it on the string without drawing. "And you're the keeper. Provisional."

The traveller came on, step and step and step, with the staff thumping the road, and stopped ten paces from the gate.

It was a woman. Grey at the temples, weathered to leather, with a face like the side of a mountain.

She looked at the gate. She looked at the new mark on the slab in the yard. She looked up, a long way up, at the platform on its new wooden frame and the beam with its fresh scarf joint.

Then she said, in a voice like a door being opened after a long time shut:

"Which of you is the keeper?"

Kael stepped out from behind the wall, and the arrow went past his ear and buried itself in the gatepost a hand's breadth from the traveller's head.

"Lyra!" he said.

"That's a warning," Lyra said, from the loophole, already drawing the second. "It's what my mother did to strangers."

The traveller had not flinched. She was looking at the arrow in the gatepost, and then at the wall, and then — slowly — she smiled. It was not a friendly smile. It was the smile of someone who has been expecting a particular bad news for years and has just been told the exact thing.

"Keeper," she said. "I've walked from the fourth stump. I'm the last one left who knows the count." She set her staff down on the road. "And I've come to tell you that the chain is going out."
