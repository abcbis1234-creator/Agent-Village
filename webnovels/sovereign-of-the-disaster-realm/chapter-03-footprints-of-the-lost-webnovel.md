# Chapter 3: Footprints of the Lost

The things in the grass did not attack until the road ended.

Kael had been walking for an hour by the light of the fire in the tower — a poor lamp but a steady one — and in that hour the sound had kept pace with him on his left. Never closer than thirty paces. Never farther than fifty.

He had decided it was a herd animal. Then wind. Then, for several minutes, a very polite group of people who did not wish to be introduced.

That last one had at least been funny.

Then the road came to a gap.

It was not a small gap.

Somewhere ahead in the dark, the embankment had given way, and a long section of road had simply slid into the gully below. He knew because the stones of the roadbed ended in a clean broken edge at the tips of his shoes, and beyond it the ground fell away into a cold, black space that smelled of wet earth.

A hundred paces of rubble and tilted slabs. Then the road again on the far side, intact and pale in the starlight, going on toward the tower as if nothing had happened.

He was still working out what to do about that when the grass behind him moved.

It moved all at once. Not the way wind moves it.

A long dark seam opened across the slope on both sides of the road, closing in from the flanks the way a net is drawn.

Kael turned in place.

At the edge of the paving, where the stones stopped and the grass began, shapes were standing up.

They were long in the leg and narrow in the body, grey as old rope. Each ribcage showed through its skin like the frame of a boat. There were perhaps a dozen. They had no eyes he could see — only a pale smear where eyes should be — and they carried their heads low and level, like things that had learned to hunt where the ground was uneven and any misstep got you killed.

None of them stepped onto the road.

He noticed that before he noticed his own heartbeat, which was a small mercy.

They stopped exactly at the kerb stones, in a line so neat it might have been ruled, and stood there, breathing.

One lifted a forefoot, held it above the first paving stone — and set it back down in the grass.

Everything below the line is trying to kill you, Hesk had said. Except the road.

Kael looked at the gap behind him. Then at the shapes in front of him. Then at the grey span of road he was standing on, which was at that moment the only dry ground in the world.

"Well," he said, in a voice that was almost level. "That's a design flaw."

He backed up.

Carefully. A step at a time, hand on the knife, no real expectation of it doing anything. The shapes didn't follow. They paced him along the grass, matched his speed, and waited at the edge of every stone as though the road were a river they had agreed not to cross.

When he stopped, they stopped.

That bought him a minute. A minute was long enough to think.

The road was safe because they wouldn't touch it. The road had a hole in it, because centuries without maintenance will do that to anything. The hole could only be crossed by leaving the paving.

The things in the grass knew this. They hadn't rushed him because they didn't need to.

They had simply been walking beside him, patiently, to the one place where he had to leave.

"Clever," Kael told them. "I hate it."

He walked back along the road, looking for something he had seen an hour ago and filed in the part of his head where he kept useful things.

It took longer than he liked. The creatures kept pace — a dozen grey frames sliding along the verge — and the sound they made was the dry sound of people being very careful.

There.

A low arch in the retaining wall on the downhill side, black against the stonework, not far above the ground. He had taken it for another drain. He knelt and put his hand inside and felt cold air moving against his palm.

A culvert. A big one. Built to carry a stream under the embankment. The embankment had come down, but the vault had been laid by someone who built to last — pitched stone, tight-jointed, the arch intact as far as his arm could reach.

If water had been cut under the road, it had to come out somewhere past the collapse.

He couldn't tell whether it was open all the way. He couldn't tell whether anything lived in it.

He could, however, tell what was behind him.

"Right," Kael said, and went in headfirst.

It was sixty-one paces. He knew because he counted — with his hands, in the dark, on his belly, the screwdriver clenched in his teeth for reasons he would not defend later.

The floor was wet and slick with silt. The vault was just high enough for a crouch. Twice the passage narrowed and he had to turn his shoulders to get through. Water ran past him ankle-deep, so cold it burned.

At the thirtieth pace the stone changed beneath his palm — from dressed to rough. He stopped. Felt along it with his thumb.

A repair.

Clay, packed and rammed between stones set by hand and not by a mason's rule. The same wandering courses he had seen on the kerb. The same careful, unskilled stubbornness.

Somebody had come down here with a basket of clay and a rammer and a lamp, and kept the water running because the road above it mattered.

They were still doing it, Kael thought. At the end. Whatever the end was.

He crawled on.

When the vault opened above him, it opened onto stars.

He came out of a stone mouth into a walled yard — on his knees in a shallow channel, soaked to the chest, shaking with cold — and for a moment did not understand what he was looking at.

It was a farmstead. Had been one.

A rectangle of dry-stone wall, twice the height of a man at its tallest, ran around a space the size of a tennis court. The roof had fallen from the long building on the north side, but its walls stood, with the dark holes of windows in them. In the middle of the yard stood a well, with a stone curb and a rotted windlass, the curb cut from a single block.

And the wall had a gap in it.

A breach about three paces wide where a section had collapsed outward. Through it he could see the grey frames of the creatures flowing around the end of the rubble field toward the opening.

They had not needed the road at all.

They had only needed to wait for him to commit.

Kael looked at the breach. He looked at the stones lying around it — weathered, squared on three faces, fallen in a heap that still showed the pattern of their courses.

Then he looked at his hands, which were shaking, and put them to work.

Dry-stone walling was not a skill he had planned on needing. But he had spent a summer, long ago and for money, rebuilding a garden wall for a man who hated everyone. The man had taught him one thing worth keeping: you did not build a wall by stacking. You built it by finding the stone that wanted to sit where you were putting it.

The first creature reached the breach as he set the third stone.

It paused in the gap. Its head came round on a long neck.

Kael did not look at it. He picked up a flat slab, turned it over, put it down across the two below, and wedged it with a fist-sized rock.

Then another.

The creature took a step in.

"I wouldn't," Kael said, and set the next stone in front of its feet.

It stopped.

He did not know why. Perhaps it was the sound of his voice. Perhaps it had not expected something so small to be so inconvenient.

He built — heart in his throat, cold pooling in his boots — and the wall went up in front of its face one course at a time, and it backed out of the gap with a low, dry hiss as the opening narrowed.

The last stone took three tries to seat.

When it went in, the wall was a little over waist-high and not pretty. It would not stop anything strong. But it was solid, the courses were bonded, and the things outside the yard stood and breathed at it in the dark, and did not come over.

He sat down against it, very slowly, because his legs had stopped taking instructions.

"Okay," Kael said to nobody. "Okay."

They tested the wall all night.

He did not sleep. He sat with his back to the stones and the knife across his knees and listened to the grey frames circle outside. Every hour or so one of them would put a forefoot on the top course and lean, and he would get up and push back with his shoulder and find the loose stone and wedge it.

By the third round he had stopped being afraid and started being tired, which he decided was an improvement. By the fifth he was fairly sure it was the same one every time — a big dark-flanked animal that tested the same corner.

He started saving a stone for it.

Toward morning, the sound changed.

It was not that they went away. It was that they stopped all at once, as though somebody had said a word.

The breathing faded. The grass settled.

When he risked a look over the top, the slope was empty, and the only things moving in the blue-grey light were the mist coming off the gully and a long line of small white birds, standing along the roof ridge of the farmhouse as if they had been there for years.

He looked at the ground outside the wall.

There were footprints.

Not the creatures'. Those he could see — a hundred overlapping scuffs in the mud, long and narrow. But in the soft earth by the corner he had been defending, almost trampled out, there was a different mark.

A boot. Light, small, narrow heel, pointed toe.

Fresh. Not more than a few hours old.

Someone had stood here while he built. Stood close, and watched, and left.

Kael crouched and followed the line of it with his eyes. It led away along the base of the wall to a thin cord of braided grass that he had missed — strung a hand's breadth off the ground between two stakes, with a cluster of pale bones tied to it.

A snare line. A boundary.

The same line ran off up the slope toward the tower, in neat, spaced posts, as clear as a surveyor's string.

Someone had spent a long time making this place hard to approach.

He stood up, slowly, and turned to look at the tower. It stood on its spur of rock a quarter mile up the valley, black and broken against the dawn. The fire in the top had gone out. A thin line of smoke still rose from it.

Something moved on the broken wall. A small shape against the sky, with one arm drawn back.

The arrow struck the top of the stone beside his hand so hard that chips stung his cheek.

"That's far enough," said a voice.
