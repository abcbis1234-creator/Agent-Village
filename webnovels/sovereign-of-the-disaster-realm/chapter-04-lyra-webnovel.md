# Chapter 4: Lyra

Kael did not move.

He had learned a great deal in the last twelve hours, and most of it had been unpleasant. But one lesson was worth keeping: when something sharp lands within a hand's width of your fingers, the correct response is to hold very still and admire the craftsmanship.

The arrow stood quivering in the top course of his wall. Ash shaft, straight-grained and oiled. Fletching cut from something grey.

Somebody had made it with care. That seemed relevant.

"That was far enough," he agreed, to the sky in general. "Thank you for the clarity."

"Hands."

He lifted both. In the right was the screwdriver, which he had been gripping since the night before without noticing. He held it out at arm's length like a man presenting evidence.

Silence from the tower. A long, suspicious one.

"What is that?" the voice said.

"A screwdriver."

"What does it do?"

"It drives screws."

"What's a screw?"

Kael considered the full answer — a threaded fastener, a pilot hole, an entire civilization's worth of hardware stores — and decided it could wait.

"It's like a nail that wants to be turned."

Another silence. Up on the broken wall, the small shape shifted its weight.

"You came into the Disaster Realm," the voice said, flat as a plank, "armed with a tool for turning nails."

"And a knife. It's blunt, though. I was told not to read anything into that."

He put the knife down on the wall beside the arrow, slowly, and stepped back.

The shape dropped out of sight.

Kael waited. He was fairly sure that was how people in books approached these moments — with stillness and good posture — and fairly sure real life paid less attention to posture than books did.

It took long enough that his legs began to remind him they had not slept. Then something moved at the base of the spur, where the slope turned to rock, and a figure came down the line of posts toward him at an easy, unhurried walk, one hand resting on the bow across her back.

She was shorter than he had expected, and older than her voice.

Hair the color of cold ash, braided tight against her skull. One ear notched near the tip in a way that did not look like an accident. Skin carrying the same grey, a little darker at the knuckles. Her coat was patched in at least five different cloths, every patch stitched flat and square.

The stitching was better than anything he had ever managed.

Her eyes were dark and quick and went over him in a single pass, taking inventory the way he took inventory of a building: roof, walls, damp.

She stopped ten paces from the wall. Exactly the distance at which a person could shoot and not be grabbed.

"You built that," she said, nodding at the stones.

"Last night."

"All of it?"

"There was a hole in the old one. They were coming through it." He glanced at the slope behind her, empty now and pale with dew. "They went away before dawn. All at once. I'd like to say that was my doing, but I suspect it wasn't."

"It wasn't." She did not say whose it was. Her gaze went to the wall again — the courses, the bonding, the wedge stones he had driven in with his fist. "Dry-laid. Through-stones. You stagger the joints."

"I had a summer job."

"With who?"

"A man who hated everyone." He thought about it. "He did excellent walls."

Something in her face moved. Not a smile — a muscle that had once been considering one.

"Name."

"Kael."

"From where?"

He hesitated. The honest answer was Earth, and he had no idea what that would do. "Not from here. I came through a summoning, four days up the road. At a place called Aurelia. They measured me. I came out with nothing, and they walked me to the edge of a cliff and left me there."

"Nothing?"

"Mana capacity, zero." The number still irritated him. "The crystal said so."

She looked at him long enough that he began to count the notches on her bowstring. Then, in a tone halfway between insult and diagnosis: "Everybody has some."

"I'm told not."

"That's not a thing that happens."

"It happened to me. I was there." He tried a small, tired smile. "I'd love a second opinion, if you know anyone."

She did not smile back. But she lowered her hand from the bow, which he took as the first real piece of progress the day had offered.

"Lyra," she said. "Don't make me regret saying it."

"What are they?" Kael asked, tipping his head toward the empty slope. "The grey ones."

"Verge-hounds." She said it like a word she had worn smooth. "They hunt the edge of things."

"What does that mean?"

"Roads. Walls. Rivers. Treelines. Anywhere one kind of ground turns into another, that's where they wait." She paused. "They don't cross the road. I've never seen why."

"That's what the soldier said."

"Then he was the only honest one you met."

She did not take him up to the tower. She took him around it, and made it clear this was not a courtesy.

"Left," she said. He went left. "Don't." He stopped with one foot in the air. After that she said nothing at all, and he understood this was the whole tour: a stranger steered past her defenses by someone with no intention of explaining them.

He worked them out anyway, which seemed to annoy her.

The snare line was not a line. It was a net — posts and cords and weighted stakes strung across the whole slope, set so anything coming up from the valley would be funneled toward a few narrow gaps.

"That's a funnel," he said.

"Keep walking."

A patch of dead grass lay ahead, a rectangle, too regular to be natural. He stepped around it half a pace before she spoke.

She stopped. "How did you see that?"

"Nothing else on this slope is dead in a straight line."

She looked at him for ten paces without saying anything, and he did not ask her to.

At the third gap a verge-hound hung in the snare. Dead for days. The grey hide had stiffened over the long ribs, and its throat had been opened with a single clean cut — the kind that came from a person who had done it before and meant to do it again.

Up close it was smaller than he had thought. Not much heavier than a large dog. Long jaw. No eyes at all.

Kael looked at the cords above it, and at the pale bones tied along them that he had taken for a charm.

They were jawbones. He counted fourteen.

"Don't touch it," Lyra said. "The others come for the dead."

"How many have you—"

"Enough."

He let that go too.

The tower stood on its spur like a broken tooth. Close up it was larger than it had seemed from the valley — thick-walled, battered at the base, built so a siege would have to climb its own footprint to get near. The top had been sheared off by something very large or very old.

A stair of outer stone climbed the north face in a long, steep diagonal. Halfway up he could see a pile of split firewood, stacked by length, the driest at the front. A beacon pit capped the highest surviving floor, blackened with years of use, a thread of smoke still curling from it.

Somebody tends that every day, he thought.

"The fire," he said. "Do you keep it going all night?"

She did not answer.

"Is it for them? The hounds?"

"No."

"Then who is it for?"

"Ask me something else."

"All right." He looked up at the broken stonework. "Who built the tower?"

Her mouth tightened. She walked on, and he understood that this, too, was an answer, and that he had spent his allowance.

He asked once more anyway, because he was tired and stubborn, and he asked it very mildly. "Is it for someone, Lyra?"

"It's for the fire," she said, in a voice with no give in it. "Leave it."

He left it. He did not believe her. But he knew the sound of a door being shut on something that hurt, and he had slammed enough of his own.

At the foot of the spur the rock opened onto a walled yard — an apron of flat ground between the tower and the cliff. A ring of old stonework enclosed it, four paces deep at the narrowest and twice that at the widest. There was a single entrance, and she stopped in the middle of it, arm across the opening, as if it were a door and she had just remembered she owned it.

"You can look from there."

In the frame, where a gate should have been, there was a heap of rotted timber and a pair of heavy iron straps, rusted through, hanging from the post like the last pieces of a skeleton.

He had meant to say something sensible. Instead he went down on one knee at the post and ran his hand over it.

Oak. Black with age but sound at the heart. The hinge pins had snapped off at the knuckle, and the strap plates came away in flakes, but the mortices were deep and square, and the lintel above had cracked without shifting.

Whoever hung the original gate had known what they were doing: a single leaf, three planks thick, cut for this frame and nothing else. It would have swung true with one hand.

"It hasn't closed in forty years," Lyra said, above him. "Maybe more. Before I was born, anyway."

"It could close."

"Not without hinges."

"I could make hinges." He heard himself say it, and it sounded a good deal more confident than he felt. "Not good ones. But the frame is sound. If you had planks, and a forge, or even a decent fire..."

"I have one fire. It isn't for gates."

"I didn't ask you to use it."

"I don't need help."

"Okay."

"I mean it."

"I believe you." He stood up. "What happens if they get in here?"

"They don't."

"That isn't what I asked."

For a moment he thought she would shoot him after all. Her hand was on the bow again, not drawn, just resting there the way another person might rest a hand on a doorknob, and under the flat voice she was holding something very carefully, like a full cup.

Then something in her shoulders let go, one degree at a time, and she looked, for the first time, tired.

"They did, once. Last winter. Four of them, through that gap." She nodded at the long low building tucked against the cliff, where a collapsed corner had been patched with a tarpaulin and a hopeful stack of stones. "They went through the stores before I woke."

She stopped there, so he did too.

He took what was left of the bread out of his bundle. A heel and a half, hard from the cold, and he had not looked at it for an hour without wanting it. He tore it, set the larger piece on top of the gatepost, and went back to looking at the lintel as if he had put nothing there.

She did not touch it. She did not look at it — which was how he knew she had been looking at it for a minute.

"How long does what's left last?" he said, to the lintel.

"Long enough."

He said nothing. The silence went on as long as he could stand to make it, and a little longer. Then she said, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing, "Eight days. If I eat less."

She took the bread, and ate it with her back half turned, the way people eat when they would rather not be seen needing to.

"And tonight?" Kael asked.

"Tonight they're coming back." She said it the way a person reads a date off a calendar. "I counted forty at dawn. Twice what I had last month. They gathered at the bottom of the slope while you were building. Something is pulling them in." She glanced at the sky, then at him. "They stopped when you stopped, at the wall. I want to know why."

"I don't know."

"Neither do I. They would have gone for you, and they didn't. So you're either very lucky or very strange, and I'm short of both."

He looked at the heap of rotten wood, and the rusted straps, and the long black post with its square-cut sockets. He thought about the road. He thought about the clay in the culvert, and the crooked mend in the kerb, and all the people who had come after the builders and had never been able to match them, and had kept the thing standing anyway.

Someone might need it tomorrow.

"Show me what you've got," he said. "Wood, rope, iron. Anything. We have until dusk."

"We?" said Lyra.

"I'm not going to stand here and watch you lose a gate," Kael said, and took off his cloak.
