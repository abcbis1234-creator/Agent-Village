# Chapter 2: The Ancient Road

The doors closed with a sound like a lid.

Cold air met Kael at the threshold. Snow. Horses. And, very far off, something burnt.

The corridor gave way to a courtyard, the courtyard to a gate, and the gate to four days of hills and one wet-eyed soldier who had stopped meeting his gaze on the second night.

Now there was only the Marches.

The wall ran east and west along the lip of a cliff for as far as he could see — waist-high, grey fieldstone, the kind of thing a farmer builds to stop sheep from leaving. Boundary posts stood along it, each with a strip of faded gold cloth tied to the top.

Behind him lay the kingdom.

In front of him, nothing but air, and a very large amount of land a very long way down.

"Cloak," said a voice.

Kael turned. A soldier stood at the back of a cart with a bundle in his arms, handling it like something that might go off. Heavy man, about fifty, a scar across the back of one hand, and the stiff patience of someone who had done a great many unpleasant jobs correctly.

"Corporal Hesk," the man said. "Cloak. Bread. Water. There's a knife in the bundle — it's blunt. Don't read anything into that."

"I won't."

"Good."

Kael took the bundle. It was heavier than it should have been. One of the other soldiers had added something, he suspected, because the others would not look at it.

He tucked the screwdriver into his belt and looked at the cart instead.

The left rear wheel had a linchpin worn down to a sliver. He had noticed it on day two. He had noticed it again on day three and told himself it wasn't his cart.

By now the wheel had a wobble you could see from across a field.

He crouched and put his thumb against the pin.

"What are you doing?" Hesk asked.

"That's going to fail somewhere around the second ford." Kael worked the pin free. There was a nail in the bundle. Wrong shape, which meant bending it, which meant ten minutes. "You'd lose the wheel, then the axle, then half a day. I'm not telling you anything you don't know."

"It isn't your problem."

"It's somebody's." He bent the nail around the end of the screwdriver, tapped it home with a stone, and gave the wheel a shove. It turned true. "There. Now it's nobody's."

Hesk looked at the wheel for a long moment. Then at Kael — with an expression that took a while to settle on anything.

"You know what they told us," he said at last. "That you were going to a death. They gave me an order to say it was a mercy."

"Was it?"

"I don't know what it is." Hesk glanced at the cliff. "I know it's below the line, and we don't go below the line. That's not an order. That's a preference of mine."

Kael followed the glance.

And for the first time, he looked properly at what he had been avoiding.

There was a road.

It began a few paces beyond the wall, where the cliff broke into a long ramp. And it went down.

Not the way a goat track goes down, in a scramble of loose stone. It went down in switchbacks, cut into the rock, each turn banked, each stretch buttressed by a retaining wall of fitted blocks. A road built by people who intended to use it for a very long time.

At the first turn it was ten paces wide. Two wagons could have passed without either driver having to be polite.

"That's the Old Road," Hesk said. "Goes all the way down. Nobody uses it. But nobody's let it fall apart, either — and there's the thing." He spat into the wind. "Keep to it. I mean that. Everything below the line is trying to kill you, except the road. I've never known why."

"Who built it?"

"Nobody knows." Hesk shrugged, the way a man shrugs when he has asked and been told to stop. "Older than the kingdom. Older than the wall, and the wall's four hundred years old."

He held out his hand. After a moment Kael understood and shook it. The grip was dry, brief, and slightly apologetic.

"If you find anyone down there," Hesk said, "tell them the Marches aren't a wall. They're a sort of apology."

Kael didn't ask what he meant.

The cart turned. The mule gave him a parting look of pure contempt. Within a quarter hour the sound of wheels had gone, and there was only wind, and the wall, and the long road falling away into afternoon.

Kael stood there longer than he meant to.

The anger arrived while he was standing. Late, like a parcel that had been misrouted. It hadn't come in the chamber — too many people, too little air. Now there was only wind, and it had room to unpack.

Measured by a rock. Judged by a man in a clean robe. Walked four days to a cliff.

Nobody had asked him a single question he could have answered.

He let it sit for the length of a long breath. Then he put it somewhere he could find it again, because it was going to be useful.

Leon, he thought. Not with anger. More like a coat left on a train — someone should check it was all right.

He put the cloak on, fastened the bundle across his back, and went over the wall.

The road was level.

That was the first thing, and it took several turns to understand why it mattered. Water ran off it into carved channels at the edge — neat, shallow, clear of everything except a little moss. The surface was laid in wedge-shaped stones, joints so tight he couldn't have slid the screwdriver blade between them.

A hundred paces down, where a rockfall had come across the track in some past winter, somebody had cleared it and left a retaining kerb standing on the cliff side. It hadn't tilted. It hadn't slumped.

Kael knelt and laid his palm flat on the stone.

Cold. Dry. The faint dull polish of ground walked on for a very long time.

Centuries, he thought. The wall was four hundred years old, and Hesk said this was older. And in all that time, nobody had maintained it. Nobody had touched it.

And it was still true to within a finger's width.

He had seen roads in his own world, built by modern crews with modern machines, breaking up within a decade because someone skimped on the base layer.

Whoever built this had not skimped on anything.

But it wasn't all the original work.

Two switchbacks down, a section of the outer kerb had failed long ago, and someone had mended it — smaller stones, set dry, a little crooked, courses wandering like a drunk's handwriting. The mend had held. Only because it had been worked at: joints packed with clay, the clay renewed more than once.

Kael ran his thumb over it.

These weren't the road's builders. These were people who came after, who knew they couldn't build a road like this, and kept it standing anyway.

So somebody had lived here long enough to need the road to last.

Which raised an uncomfortable question: why build something this good for a place everyone called a prison?

He walked on, and the question walked with him. It didn't get smaller.

Ten turns down, he stopped counting the switchbacks and started counting the small things instead. A drainage culvert cut through the cliff, lined with dressed stone, still draining. A shelf of flat rock at exactly the height a tired traveler would want to sit. A line of low pillars along the outer edge, spaced exactly twelve paces apart, each with a socket at the top where something had once been fitted.

Lamps, he thought. Or flags. Or a rope.

You didn't put sockets in pillars for a road you expected no one to use.

The light changed. The sky over the valley turned the color of old brass, and long shadows came up the slope toward him.

He ate half the bread without noticing, then made himself stop and wrap the rest.

Down below, the land opened into a long, shallow basin — grey-green and silent, cut by the pale thread of the road. There were shapes in it. Low humps and rectangles. Lines too straight to be natural.

Fields, he realized.

Those had been fields.

Not wild land at all. Ground that had been worked and fenced and drained, and left.

Something in his chest turned over again, quieter this time, and for no reason he could name.

He kept walking.

He found the milestone just before dusk.

It stood at a bend where the road leveled out for the first time. At first he took it for another pillar — a stub of grey stone about as high as his hip. Then he saw the carving.

The face was weathered almost smooth, but the cuts were deep. Lines of small, tidy marks ran in two columns. Nothing he recognized. He ran a finger down the first column and felt the grooves, cleaner than the rest of the stone, as though the carver had gone back over them.

Distances, probably. People carved distances.

Above the columns, nearly worn away, there was a symbol.

A star. Very even. Seven points.

He stared at it without knowing why. It was only a star. Stars were common.

But the points were spaced with exactly the same care as the pillars.

He took out the screwdriver, absurdly, then put it back. He was not going to scratch his name on a road.

Behind him, a long way up, the first stars were showing over the Marches. He hadn't realized it was so late.

Right, Kael thought. Shelter.

He had a cloak, half a loaf, a blunt knife, a waterskin a third full, and a road built by people who knew what they were doing.

Then he stopped.

There was a sound.

Somewhere below and to the left, at the edge of hearing: a low, uneven noise, like a great many things moving carefully through dry grass.

It stopped when he stopped.

He took a step. It began again — patient, unhurried, keeping pace.

Kael stood very still. Hesk's words came back to him: keep to the road.

He was keeping to the road.

The noise went on, and he began to understand that it didn't matter.

Then, far off down the valley in the last grey light, something else caught his eye.

A tower — or what was left of one — stood alone on a spur of rock a mile ahead. Its top was broken. Its walls were black with age.

And in the broken top, small and orange and steady, a fire was burning.

Somebody was down here.

Somebody had lit that fire, and kept it lit, and hadn't been killed by anything yet.

Kael took a breath, shifted the bundle on his back, and walked toward it.
