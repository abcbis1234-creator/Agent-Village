# Chapter 9: The Storm

It held for eleven minutes.

Kael counted them, because counting was the only thing left that felt like work. He stood braced in the lee of the beacon stone with his shoulder against the cold masonry and Lyra's sleeve wound twice around his fist, and the storm went over the tower like a freight train that had decided to take its time.

The rain was no longer rain. It was a solid thing thrown at the face, with grit in it. Every few seconds the lightning cracked white across the valley and showed him the whole scene in a single flash: the black slope, the thrashing grass, the pale thread of the road, the fire lying flat in its pit like a dog hiding from a blow.

At minute nine he heard the gate.

It was a small sound under all the others. A groan from far below, from the foot of the tower. But he had listened to that pin turn in its socket for three days, and the note was wrong. It had gone up half a tone — the way a bearing sounds just before it seizes.

"Lyra."

"I know." She had her mouth against his ear. "Look at the floor."

He looked.

The beacon platform was a ring of old stone laid across a single huge timber — a beam that ran the width of the broken tower and carried everything above it. Along the beam's underside, where the stone met the wood, there was a line of dark water.

He had taken it for rain.

Then the next flash of lightning came, and he saw it was moving: a crack, hair-fine, opening and closing with the wind like a mouth.

"That's the platform," he said.

"That's the platform." Her face was a pale smear in the dark, hair plastered to it. "And that's the gate. And we have one of each."

He understood what she was saying a second before she said it, and wished that he had not.

The gate needed someone with a wedge and a shoulder and the sense to know when the pivot was about to give. The platform needed someone to hold a prop under the beam and keep the fire alive until the wind dropped.

It was not a hard sum. Each job would fail without a person. There were two people.

"We split," Kael said.

"Yes."

"For the length of the storm."

"For the length of the storm." She reached up and took hold of his collar, hard, and shook it once. "Two people. I know. I know what I said. Go and hold your gate."

He did not say anything clever. It was the first time in a week he had not thought of something clever to say.

He put his hand over hers on his collar for the length of a breath, and then went down the stair into the dark.

The gate was fighting him before he reached it.

He could see why. The wind had swung round to the north-east and was hitting the leaf at an angle the builders had not planned for. Every gust lifted the great oak planks a finger's breadth against the pin and dropped them again. The socket stone was shifting in its bed. A thin line of silt had worked up around the rim, and in the flashes of lightning it looked like blood.

He wedged the leaf with the oak shim. The shim split.

He tried a second, and it held for a minute. He put his shoulder against the planks and took the weight of every gust through his collarbone, rocking with it the way you ride a boat.

A piece of slate went past his head and shattered on the wall. His hands were numb. He kept finding that he was counting again.

*It was meant to close itself,* he thought. *It was meant to be pushed by weather.* The builders had known this wind. They had hung the gate so that a storm would seat it rather than break it, and he had not understood that, and he felt a stab of shame at the thought of all the careful people who had come and gone and known so much more than he did.

So he stopped fighting it.

He let the gate move. He eased the shim back by half and let the leaf take the gust the way it wanted to — against the stop, with the weight going down into the stone — and the groan under his hand dropped back to its old note.

It held.

He was laughing, a little wildly, with his forehead against the oak, when the stair went.

He did not see it go. He heard it: a crack like a rifle shot, and then a long, tearing shriek that went on much too long — the sound of a great many pegs leaving a great many mortices at once.

He spun round. The lightning came, a white bar from sky to ground, and in its light he saw the whole structure he had spent three days building lift away from the wall of the tower like a page being turned.

Thirty treads. Oak, pegged, planed by Lyra's own hands.

It rose, and hung for an instant in the air, and folded.

Then the dark closed over it, and the wind took the sound.

"Lyra!"

He shouted it again. The wind made a mockery of it. He might as well have shouted at the sea.

He ran. His legs went out from under him on the wet stone and he went down hard, and got up, and ran again, to the foot of the tower.

There was nothing there.

The stair had been torn from the wall and flung, and it lay across the yard in a spray of splintered oak — a few treads still pegged together, a rail snapped at the middle. The base of the tower stood naked and black.

Above it, forty feet of wet masonry went up into the dark.

And at the top, a little orange light flickered.

Then it went out.

It happened between one breath and the next. The fire that had burned on that platform every night for as long as Lyra had been alive guttered, and sank, and was not there. A chunk of stone fell from the rim of the tower, and he heard it hit the cliff on the way down.

The crack in the beam had opened.

Nobody on the platform was shouting. Nobody on the platform was doing anything at all.

*She's up there,* he thought. *Or she's down there.*

He did not know which, and the not knowing was a physical thing, like a nail driven in under the ribs.

He had no way up. The stair was gone, and the rope ladder — which hung from the platform's edge — was a dark thread twisting in the wind a body's length above the rubble.

The gate was behind him. The gate was fine.

*Stay. It's the job.*

He thought of her hand on his collar. *I know what I said.*

He went for the ladder.

He could not remember much of the climb afterward.

He remembered the rubble, the way a beam of the broken stair shifted under his knee and tried to roll him. He remembered the jump — which was not a jump so much as a lurch, a flat grab at a wet rope with both hands, and the burn of it as it ran through his palms.

He remembered the wind catching him halfway and slamming him against the tower, and a bright, flat crack inside his chest that did not feel like pain at first — only like a door being kicked shut.

After that there was just the ladder, and the knots, and the count.

He reached the platform on his belly. The stone rim was gone in places, torn away in ragged bites, and the beam under it bowed like a bad tooth. Half the fire pit lay in the dark, scattered over the edge.

Lyra sat against the beacon stone with her coat over her head and her back to the wind. She had a long prop wedged under the beam — wedged badly — and her hands were around it, and her knuckles were white.

She looked up.

"You left the gate," she said.

"It's fine."

"You left the gate."

"I know." He crawled to her, and sat, and found that he could not get his breath all the way in. "It's holding. I wedged it. Lyra, the stair..."

"I saw." Her face was wet. Some of it was rain. She was looking at him as though he had done something astonishing and idiotic, and she was not sure which she wanted to say first. "It went. And the fire went. And I thought... you were at the bottom..."

"I wasn't under it."

"I know that now."

He reached out. She reached out. Neither one could have said afterward who moved first.

They each found the other's sleeve in the dark, and took hold, and did not say anything else, because there was not anything left that could have been said.

They held on until dawn.

The storm blew itself out slowly, as storms do — the wind dropping by degrees, the rain thinning to a grey drizzle and then to nothing. The lightning moved off to the south.

At some point he realised that he could hear the creak of the gate, down in the yard, and that it was the sound of a gate that was still on its pivot.

The light came up pale and washed.

Before she got to her feet, Lyra took hold of his wrists and turned his hands palm-up, and looked at what the rope had done to them. She said a word he had not heard her use before.

Then she tore a strip off the hem of her coat, and wrapped each palm with the quick, flat stitching of somebody who had done it many times, and tied the ends off with her teeth.

"Don't use them for a day."

"I need to use them for everything."

"Then use them badly." She let go. She stood, and stood looking down.

The yard was a mess. Planks everywhere, a sheet of the store roof lifted and thrown. But the roof itself stood. The storehouse stood. The well stood, with its curb.

And the gate — scarred along one edge, scraped bare where a flying plank had hit it — stood in its frame as if nothing had happened.

At the foot of the tower, where the stair had been, there was a pile of kindling.

"Our stair," Lyra said. She said it quietly, to nobody, in a flat small voice. "It would have held in half that."

"It would."

"We should have braced it."

"We didn't have the rope."

"We should have braced it," she said again, and her voice cracked, and she put the heel of her hand against her eye, and stood very still.

Then she turned, and looked at the beacon pit.

It was black. Half the ring had fallen away. In the ruins of the other half lay a scatter of wet ash and blackened ends, and under it — where the slab had been tipped by the beam's collapse — a glimpse of pale carved stone.

She knelt beside it. She did not say a word. She laid her palm on the cold ash, and kept it there.

He left her to it.

He went down the rope ladder one rung at a time, because his ribs would not do it any faster, and sat at the foot of the tower with his back against the wall and his burned hands in his lap.

He was shaking. Distantly he could tell that something in his chest was wrong.

But the sun was coming up, and the gate was open to the morning, and there was no sound in the valley but the dripping of water from the broken stone.

He rested his hand on the ground beside the ledger stone. His coat was gone, taken by the wind, and the slab lay bare in its hollow.

It was not cold.

It was not warm.

It was hot — as hot as a stove lid — with a dull pulse in it, like something under the stone had finally woken up and was breathing.
