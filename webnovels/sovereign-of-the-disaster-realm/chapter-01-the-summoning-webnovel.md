# Chapter 1: Zero Mana

Kael Arden had one rule for a job: leave it better than you found it.

He was on his knees in Mrs. Okafor's kitchen, screwdriver between his teeth, when the floor turned to gold.

It came up through his shoes first — a heat with no smell and no sound — and then the light followed, climbing his legs, swallowing the stairwell, the dented mailboxes, the flickering bulb, the whole tired world one piece at a time.

He had time for exactly one clear thought.

*Well. This probably wasn't covered by my insurance.*

Then the gold went out like a lamp someone had remembered to switch off.

Kael was kneeling on stone.

The floor was white, veined with gold, polished so clean that the lamps overhead lay across it in long, broken ribbons. The ceiling was too high for the room. Painted figures covered it, robed and solemn, their hands raised toward a painted sun.

Around him stood thirty or so men and women in white and gold.

They were staring at him like a parcel they had ordered and did not expect to be wet.

He was still holding the cabinet door.

That was the first thing he checked, because it was the only thing he could check. Cheap pine, one hinge dangling. He had told Mrs. Okafor ten minutes, and he was a man who meant it.

He set the door down very carefully, as though that might help.

"Hello," he said. "Is this the right apartment?"

Nobody laughed.

A woman in the front row put a hand over her mouth. It was not to hide a smile.

"There's someone else," said a voice beside him. It was shaking.

Kael turned.

A second person knelt a few feet away, inside the same chalk-white circle. A young man about his age, grey hooded jacket, pale as paper, breathing like he'd run the whole way here. His hands were flat on the floor, palms down, as if checking that the ground was real.

He had no cabinet door. He had a phone, and the screen read *No Service*, which at least was honest.

"Are you okay?" Kael asked.

The young man stared at him. People in trouble are strange about kindness. They look at it first like it might be a trick.

Then he nodded, shaky. "I think so. I was at the bus stop. There was a light, and then—" He gestured at the ceiling, the robes, the soldiers standing behind them. "Leon. I'm Leon."

"Kael." He started to offer the hand with the screwdriver in it, realized his mistake, switched to the empty one. Leon's palm was cold and damp, and he held on a moment longer than a handshake needed, the way you hold a railing on a boat.

"Whatever this is," Kael said quietly, "we'll find out together. Stay where I can see you."

Leon laughed once, short and cracked. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"Low bar."

"Very low."

"Silence."

The room folded around the word.

He came down from a dais at the far end — an old man in robes so white they were almost blue, a staff in one hand, a rope of gold chain across his chest. Two attendants followed, one carrying a cushion. On the cushion sat a crystal the size of a loaf of bread.

Kael had spent twenty-six years reading what a smile cost the person wearing it. This one was expensive. Polished like the floor.

"Welcome," the old man said, "to the Kingdom of Aurelia. I am Archmage Orlen Veyd, Keeper of the Sunward Court. You have been called across the veil by the Rite of Heroes, which has not been performed in living memory. You will have questions. Please be patient. All of them will be answered."

Kael noticed he had said *all of them*, and not *any of them*. He noticed nobody had said sorry, either.

"Called," Kael repeated. "As in, on purpose."

"As in, by prophecy." Veyd's smile did not move. "The old powers are waking in the east. The Court has long held that when the hour came, the heavens would send those who could meet it. You are that answer. Both of you."

*Both of you.* Kael filed that away.

"Before we explain further," Veyd said, "there is a custom. Each who is called is measured by the Heartstone, so we may know what the heavens have granted. It is a formality." He gestured. "The one with the jacket first."

Leon looked at Kael. Kael nodded. Leon rose on legs that clearly did not want to, crossed three steps of white stone, and put his hand on the crystal.

The crystal woke.

It did not flicker. It *opened*. Light poured out in layers — white, then gold, then a deep clean blue — and the whole chamber leaned toward it as one body.

A pane of pale script unfolded in the air above Leon's head.

**Name: Leon**
**Mana Capacity: 8,400**
**Affinity: Light (Radiant)**
**Rank: Hero-Class**

Somebody dropped a staff. It rang on the stone for a long time.

"Eight thousand," breathed a man to Kael's left, like a prayer.

Leon stood in the middle of it, lit from within, his phone still clutched in his free hand. He looked like a man who'd won a lottery he never entered, announced by someone who might want something back. He glanced at Kael.

Kael gave him a small shrug. *Don't look at me. I brought a cabinet door.*

Veyd pressed a hand to his own chest, warmth turned all the way up. "The heavens are generous. Hero Leon, you are welcome in this house." He turned. "And now the second."

The turn took a little longer than it should have.

Kael stood.

Every step across that floor rang. He made it to the crystal and looked down. Up close it was not as beautiful. There was a hairline flaw running through its heart, patched with something that wasn't the same material.

Somebody had repaired this thing a long time ago. And not very well.

"Hand on it," Veyd said, gently.

Kael put his hand on the crystal.

It was warm, like skin. For a second, nothing happened.

Then the world went very quiet.

He saw a gate.

A great wooden gate, standing open in a wall of old stone. Beyond it, a city he did not recognize, its lamps lit. People in the street. A great many of them. And they were not all people. Tall ones. Small ones. Some with shoulders that did not belong on a person.

All of them were facing him.

None of them looked afraid.

Something in his chest turned over — the way it does on the last stair of a building you've lived in for years.

One second. Then it was gone.

A pane of script unfolded above his head.

**Name: Kael Arden**
**Mana Capacity: 0**

He waited for the rest.

There was no rest. No affinity. No rank. The pane hung there, two lines long, and then, almost politely, it faded.

Kael stared at the space where the number had been.

It had not improved under observation. Which was unfortunate, because observation had been his entire plan.

"Is that," he said, "the whole thing?"

Nobody answered.

The silence in the chamber had changed. It had been the silence of a crowd holding its breath for something wonderful. Now it was the silence of a crowd realizing it had been standing in the wrong line.

Veyd's smile was still on. It had just stopped being *for him*.

"Again," Veyd said.

Kael put his hand back on the crystal. Warm. Nothing.

"Again."

The same. A third time, the old man's own palm pressed down over the back of Kael's hand, and the crystal flared bright enough to make Kael's eyes water — and gave exactly the same two lines.

"It must be a fault in the stone," someone said, hopefully.

"The stone measured the Hero perfectly," said another voice, harder. "How does a rite call *nothing*?"

Kael did not turn.

The question stayed in his chest like a swallowed coin. *Why me? Why two? And what, for one second, was it trying to show me?*

"Archmage."

Leon had walked back across the white floor. Nobody had told him to. Nobody stopped him either, because he was eight thousand mana of Radiant hero, and a person with that much light in him is hard to stop.

He stood beside Kael. His hands were shaking. He put one of them, deliberately, on Kael's shoulder.

"There's got to be a mistake. We came in together. If there's a problem with the reading, we do it again tomorrow. He can stay with me. He's—" His voice cracked, then steadied. "He asked if I was okay. First thing. Before he knew who I was. I'd want him here."

The kindness in it was real. Kael could tell. That was what made it hurt — distant and clean, like a splinter you can see and haven't pulled yet.

Veyd looked at Leon, and for a moment the warmth in his face was real too.

It had simply never been aimed at Kael.

"Hero Leon. Your heart does you credit." He laid a hand over Leon's, lifted it from Kael's shoulder, and held it between both of his, the way one does with a child. "But the Heartstone does not make mistakes. The Court cannot house what the heavens did not grant. A summons without mana cannot be sustained within the sanctum. The wards would begin to feed on him. We would be doing him no kindness."

"Then fix the wards."

"One does not fix a sanctum, Hero." A small, regretful smile. "One keeps it."

It was very well done. Kael admired it the way you admire a skilled thief. Every sentence was reasonable. Every sentence closed a door.

"What happens to me?" Kael said.

He said it quietly. It was the first thing anyone in the room had asked that sounded like a person.

Veyd turned to him. "You will be given food and a cloak. The Court is not without mercy. An escort will take you to the edge of the Marches, where the kingdom's protection ends. And there you will be released into the world." He paused. "Past the Marches, the roads are poor. I would not linger."

"And Leon?"

"Remains here. Is trained. Becomes, I trust, everything the heavens meant."

"That's it." Kael looked down at his hand, still faintly warm, and at the screwdriver he was still holding. It was an absurd thing to have brought. "I got dragged out of a stairwell. I didn't ask to come. And your solution is to walk me to the edge of the map and leave me there."

Veyd's smile stopped.

For the first time, something real flickered across his face. Not anger. A faint calculation — a man noticing a crack in a wall he had assumed was sound.

Then it was gone. Two soldiers had Kael by the arms.

They were not rough. That was the worst of it. They were polite, with the careful hands of men who had done this before and learned that politeness made it easier.

They steered him toward the far doors — the ones the whole room had been built around.

Behind him, someone was shouting. Leon. Saying his name. Saying *wait*. Saying *this isn't right* in a voice that had never said anything like it before.

The robes closed around Leon, murmuring, easing him back toward the light — like a bird herded into a cage with the door left open.

Kael did not look back. He already knew what was on Leon's face. It was easier to hate the room without it.

At the doors, he turned his head. Just once. Not to the room.

Up on the ceiling, the painted figures lifted their hands to the sun. Every face was turned upward.

Not one of them had thought to look at the floor — where the man who would have told them it was cracked was being led out.

Cold air hit him at the threshold. It smelled of snow, and horses, and — very far off — something burnt.

Kael tightened his grip on the screwdriver.

Zero, he thought. Fine.

Then he went out, and the doors closed behind him with a sound like a lid.

---

The corridor beyond was long, and cold, and lit by no lamp he could see.

The two soldiers walked him in silence. Kael counted the turns. Four left. Two right. A stair that went down instead of up, which he did not like at all.

At the fifth turn, he felt it.

A warmth in his palm.

He looked down. The screwdriver was glowing.

Not much. A thin gold line down the steel, like a seam of light in a stone. It pulsed, once, slowly — like something under the metal had finally woken up and was breathing.

The soldiers did not notice. They were looking straight ahead.

And then, in the air above his own hand — small, white, patient, visible to no one but him — a line of script unfolded.

**Sovereign Candidate: Unverified.**
**Calibration: 0%.**

Kael stopped walking.

The soldiers pulled him forward. He let them. His heart was going like a hammer on a nail, and his mind was very, very quiet.

*Unverified.*

Not *denied*.

He looked at the glowing screwdriver in his fist, and at the long dark corridor ahead, and for the first time since the floor turned gold, he smiled.

"Okay," he said under his breath. "Let's see what needs fixing."

The doors at the end of the corridor opened onto night.

Wind. A wall. A cliff.

And below the cliff, very far down, a road.
