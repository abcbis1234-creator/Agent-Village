## §2 — Story Bible

### §2.1 Premise (v1 source)

Kael Arden, a repairman with zero mana, is summoned to Aurelia, rejected by the kingdom, and exiled into the Disaster Realm. Rather than becoming a conqueror, he becomes a keeper, restorer, and builder.

### §2.2 Premise (v2 — Legendary Upgrade)

The Disaster Realm is a wound in the world; Kael is the only person alive who can suture it. Exile is quarantine; the kingdom feared a zero-mana keeper would restore what they could not bear to remember. Promise: the only one the gods exiled is the only one who can repair the end of the world. Hook: "They exiled the only man who could repair the end of the world — and called it mercy."

### §2.3 Core Themes (v1, with v2 addition)

Responsibility not ownership; restoration over destruction; maintenance as heroism; community; no one carries a weight alone.
v2 adds: stewardship across generations.

### §2.4 Characters (v1, with v2 deepening)

- Kael Arden: practical builder and future Sovereign Candidate. v2 deepened: zero mana is an ontology rather than a deficiency; his flaw is that he restores to avoid deciding; his Sovereignty path is the choice to know when repair must become rule.
- Lyra: watchpost keeper and beacon tender. v2 deepened: the beacon tender tends the chain that is the realm's hope infrastructure; her stake is that she kept the last beacon burning alone through the exile.
- Leon: Hero-Class summon with Radiant affinity. v2 deepened: Hero-Class is a burden with expectations; Radiant affinity is raw power without maintenance.

## §5 — Full Chapter Texts and Summaries

### Chapter 1: The Summoning — Full Text

"The floor exploded in gold.
It came up through Kael's shoes first, a heat with no smell and no sound, and then the light followed it, climbing his legs and shutting the stairwell, the dented mailboxes and the flickering bulb out of the world one at a time. He had time for one clear thought.
Well. This probably wasn't covered by his insurance.
Then he was kneeling on stone, and the gold went out like a lamp someone had remembered to turn off.
He was still holding the cabinet door. That was the first thing he checked, because it was the only thing he could check: the cheap pine door he had unscrewed from Mrs. Okafor's kitchen forty seconds ago, one hinge dangling, the screwdriver still in his other fist. He had told her ten minutes, and he was a man who meant it.
He set the door down very carefully, as though that might help.
The floor was white stone veined with gold. Somebody polished it often enough that the lamps overhead lay across it in long, broken ribbons, and Kael disliked it immediately. Floors that clean came with rules, and with people who got upset when you broke them. Above the floor there was a ceiling much too high for the room's width, painted with robed figures holding up their hands at a sun, and around the edge of the circle he knelt in stood thirty or so men and women in white and gold, all looking at him with the same expression. It was the look people give a parcel they ordered and did not expect to be wet.
Behind them were soldiers in good armor, standing like men who had not been told to relax.
"Hello," Kael said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted, so he tried again. "Is this the right apartment?"
Nobody laughed. A woman in the front row put her hand over her mouth, and it was not to hide a smile.
"There's someone else."
The voice came from beside him, and it was shaking.
Kael turned. A second person knelt a few feet away inside the same chalk-white circle, a young man about his own age in a grey hooded jacket, pale in the face, breathing as though he had run the whole way here. His hands were open on the floor, palms down, like someone checking that the ground was real. He had no cabinet door and no screwdriver. He had a phone, lit in one fist, and the screen read No Service, which at least was honest.
"Are you okay?" Kael asked.
The young man stared at him. People in trouble are strange about kindness; they look at it first as though it might be a trick. Then he gave a shaky nod.
"I think so. I was at the bus stop. There was a light, and then..." He gestured at the ceiling, the robes, the soldiers, all of it. "Leon," he added, a beat late, as though remembering that people did this. "I'm Leon."
"Kael." He held out the hand that had the screwdriver in it, realized his mistake, switched to the empty one. Leon took it. His palm was cold and damp, and he held on a moment longer than a handshake needed, the way you hold the railing on a boat. "Whatever this is," Kael said quietly, "we'll find out together. Stay where I can see you."
Leon laughed once, short and cracked. "That's the nicest thing anyone has said to me all week."
"Low bar."
"Very low."
"Silence."
The room folded around the word, the way a room does around a man who has never needed to raise his voice.
He came down from a dais at the far end, an old man in robes so white they were slightly blue, with a staff topped by a clear stone and a face arranged with great care into something warm. A rope of gold chain lay across his chest. Two attendants followed him, one carrying a cushion, and on the cushion sat a crystal the size of a loaf of bread.
Kael had learned, over twenty-six years and several landlords, to read what a smile cost the person wearing it. This one was expensive, and polished like the floor.
"Welcome," said the old man, "to the Kingdom of Aurelia. I am Archmage Orlen Veyd, Keeper of the Sunward Court. You have been called across the veil by the Rite of Heroes, which has not been performed in living memory. You will have questions. Please be patient. All of them will be answered."
Kael noticed that he had said all of them and not any of them. He noticed that nobody had said sorry, either.
"Called," Kael repeated. "As in, on purpose."
"As in, by prophecy." Veyd's smile did not move. "The kingdom stands at a turning. The old powers are waking in the east, and the Sunward Court has long held that when the hour comes, the heavens would send those who could meet it. You are that answer, both of you. I realize the shock is considerable."
Both of you. Kael filed that away, along with the fact that the soldiers had not once looked at the door. He knew nothing about summonings and a lot about buildings, and a room built with a single exit expected something to try to leave.
"Before the Court can explain further," Veyd went on, "there is a custom. Each who is called is measured by the Heartstone, so that we may know what the heavens have granted. It is a formality. Please, young sir. The one with the jacket first."
Leon looked at Kael. Kael nodded, because it seemed like what a person should do, and Leon rose on legs that clearly didn't want to, crossed three steps of white stone, and put his hand on the crystal because an old man in a robe told him to.
The crystal woke.
It did not flicker. It opened. Light poured out of it in layers, white and then gold and then a deep clean blue, and the whole chamber leaned toward it as one body. The robed figures on the ceiling threw long shadows across the painted sun. The woman in the front row said "Oh," softly, like someone watching a child take its first steps.
A pane of pale script unfolded in the air above Leon's head, and every person in the room could read it.
Name: Leon Mana Capacity: 8,400 Affinity: Light (Radiant) Rank: Hero-Class
Somebody dropped a staff. It rang on the stone for a long time.
"Eight thousand," breathed a man to Kael's left, like a prayer.
Leon stood in the middle of it, lit from within, with his phone still clutched in his free hand. He looked like a man who had been told he'd won a lottery he had never entered, by someone who might want something in return. He glanced back at Kael. His face asked a question Kael could answer only with a small shrug. Don't look at me. I brought a cabinet door.
Veyd had put a hand to his own chest, the warmth in his face turned all the way up. "The heavens are generous," he said. "Truly generous. Hero Leon, you are welcome in this house. And now the second."
He turned, and the turning took a little longer than it should have.
Kael stood. He had a bad feeling about the walk across that floor, and the bad feeling was right, because every step rang. He made it to the crystal and looked down at it. Up close it was not as beautiful as it had been from the circle. There was a hairline flaw running through its heart, patched, he thought, with something that wasn't the same material. Somebody had repaired this thing a long time ago and not very well.
He almost said so. Then he looked at the faces around him and decided it wasn't the moment.
"Hand on it," said Veyd, gently.
Kael put his hand on the crystal.
It was warm, like skin. For a second nothing happened, and then the world went very quiet.
He saw a gate.
It was like looking up from a page and catching something at the edge of the eye: a great wooden gate, standing open, in a wall of old stone, and on the far side of it a city he did not recognize with its lamps lit. There were people in the street, a great many of them, and they were not all people. Tall ones. Small ones. Some with shoulders that did not belong on a person. All of them were facing him, and none of them looked afraid. Something in his chest turned over, the way it does on the last stair of a building you've lived in for years.
One second. Then it was gone, and the crystal under his hand was only a crystal.
A pane of script unfolded above his head.
Name: Kael Arden Mana Capacity: 0
He waited for the rest.
There was no rest. No affinity, no rank, no second line. The pane hung there, two lines long, white on white, and then, almost politely, it faded.
Kael stared at the number. Then at the crystal. Then at the number again, though it had already left. It had not improved under observation, which was unfortunate, because observation had been his entire plan.
"Is that," he said, "the whole thing?"
Nobody answered. The silence in the chamber had changed. It had been the silence of a crowd holding its breath for something wonderful. Now it was the silence of a crowd realizing it has been standing in the wrong line.
Veyd's smile was still on. It had just stopped being for him.
"Again," said Veyd.
Kael put his hand back on the crystal. It was warm. It did nothing.
"Again."
The same. A third time, the old man's own palm over the back of Kael's hand, pressing down, and the crystal flared, brightly this time, so that Kael's eyes watered, and gave exactly the same two lines.
"It must be a fault in the stone," someone said from the crowd, hopefully.
"The stone measured the Hero perfectly," said another voice, harder. "How does a rite call nothing?"
Kael did not turn. The question stayed in his chest like a swallowed coin, because it was a good one. Why had the thing that dragged him across a veil picked him? Why two? And what, for one second, had the crystal been trying to show him?
"Archmage," said Leon.
He had walked back across the white floor. Nobody had told him to, and nobody stopped him either, because he was eight thousand mana of Radiant hero and a person with that much light in him is difficult to stop. He stood beside Kael. His hands were shaking, and he put one of them, deliberately, on Kael's shoulder.
"There's got to be a mistake. We came in together. If there's a problem with the reading, we do it again tomorrow. He can stay with me. He's—" His voice cracked, then steadied. "He asked if I was okay. First thing. Before he knew who I was. I'd want him here."
The kindness in it was real. Kael could tell. That was what made it hurt, in a distant, clean sort of way, like a splinter you can see and haven't pulled yet.
Veyd looked at Leon, and for a moment the warmth in his face was real too. It had simply never been aimed at Kael to begin with.
"Hero Leon. Your heart does you credit." He laid a hand over Leon's, lifted it from Kael's shoulder, and held it between both of his, as one does with a child. "But the Heartstone does not make mistakes, and the Court cannot house what the heavens did not grant. A summons without mana cannot be sustained within the sanctum. The wards would begin to feed on him. We would be doing him no kindness."
"Then fix the wards."
"One does not fix a sanctum, Hero." A small, regretful smile. "One keeps it."
It was very well done. Kael admired it the way you admire a skilled thief. Every sentence was reasonable, and every sentence closed a door.
"What happens to me," Kael said.
He said it quietly, and it was the first thing anybody in the room had asked that sounded like a person.
Veyd turned to him. "You will be given food and a cloak. The Court is not without mercy. An escort will take you to the edge of the Marches, where the kingdom's protection ends, and you will be released into the world." He paused. "Past the Marches the roads are poor. I would not linger."
"And Leon?"
"Remains here. Is trained. Becomes, I trust, everything the heavens meant."
"That's it." Kael looked down at his hand, still faintly warm, and at the screwdriver he was still holding. It was an absurd thing to have left. "I got dragged out of a stairwell. I didn't ask to come. And your solution is to walk me to the edge of the map and leave me there."
Veyd's smile stopped, finally, and for the first time something real flickered across his face: not anger, only a faint calculation, like a man noticing a crack in a wall he had assumed was sound.
Then it was gone, and two soldiers had Kael by the arms.
They were not rough. That was the worst of it. They were polite, with the careful hands of men who had done this before and found that politeness made it easier. They steered him down the white stone toward the far doors, the ones the room had been built around. Behind him someone was shouting. It was Leon, saying his name, saying wait, saying this isn't right in a voice that had never said anything like it before. The robes closed around Leon, murmuring, easing him back toward the light like a bird herded into a cage with the door left open.
Kael did not look back. He already knew what was on Leon's face, and it was easier to hate the room without it.
At the doors he did turn his head. Just once, and not to the room.
Up on the ceiling, the painted figures lifted their hands to the sun. Every face was turned upward, and not one of them had thought to look at the floor, where the man who would have told them it was cracked was being led out.
Cold air met him at the threshold. It smelled of snow and horses and, very far off, of something burnt.
Kael tightened his grip on the screwdriver, the only thing he had brought into this world on purpose.
Zero, he thought. Fine.
Then he went out, and the doors closed behind him with a sound like a lid.

### Chapter 2: The Ancient Road — Full Text

"The wall at the Marches had been built to keep something in, and Kael spent his first ten minutes there working out which side he was supposed to be on.
It was not much of a wall. It ran east and west along the lip of a cliff for as far as he could see, waist-high and made of grey fieldstone, the kind of thing a farmer builds to stop sheep from leaving. A line of boundary posts stood beside it, each with a strip of faded gold cloth tied to the top. Behind him lay four days of hills, snow and one wet-eyed soldier who had stopped meeting his gaze on the second night. In front of him there was nothing at all, except air, and a very large amount of land a very long way down.
The escort cart stood at the edge of the track with its tail to the cliff. There were four soldiers, a corporal named Hesk and a mule that disliked everyone equally. Hesk was unloading a bundle from the cart with the care of a man handling something that might go off.
"Cloak," he said, holding it out. "Bread. Water. There's a knife in the bundle, it's blunt, don't read anything into that."
"I won't."
"Good."
Kael took the bundle. It was heavier than it should have been, and he suspected one of the soldiers had added something, because the others would not look at it. He tucked the screwdriver into his belt and looked at the cart instead.
The left rear wheel had a linchpin worn down to a sliver. He had noticed it on day two, and again on day three, and had told himself it wasn't his cart. By now the wheel had a slight wobble you could see from across a field.
He crouched and put his thumb against the pin.
"What are you doing?" Hesk asked.
"That's going to fail somewhere around the second ford." Kael worked the pin free. There was a nail in the bundle. It was the wrong shape, which meant he would have to bend it, which meant ten minutes. "You'd lose the wheel, then the axle, then half a day. I'm not telling you anything you don't know."
"It isn't your problem."
"It's somebody's." He bent the nail round the end of the screwdriver, tapped it home with a stone, and gave the wheel a shove. It turned true. "There. Now it's nobody's."
Hesk looked at the wheel for a long moment, and then at Kael, with an expression that took a while to settle on anything. He was a heavy man of about fifty, with a scar across the back of one hand and the stiff patience of someone who had done a great many unpleasant jobs correctly.
"You know what they told us," he said at last. "That you were going to a death. They gave me an order to say it was a mercy."
"Was it?"
"I don't know what it is." Hesk glanced at the cliff. "I know that it's below the line, and we don't go below the line. That's not an order. That's a preference of mine."
Kael followed the glance, and for the first time looked properly at what he had been avoiding.
There was a road.
It began a few paces beyond the wall, where the cliff broke into a long ramp, and it went down. It did not go down the way a goat track does, in a scramble of loose stone. It went down in switchbacks, cut into the rock, each turn banked and each stretch buttressed by a retaining wall of fitted blocks, a road built by people who intended to use it for a very long time. At the first turn it was at least ten paces wide. Two wagons could have passed on it without either driver having to be polite.
"That's the Old Road," Hesk said. "It goes all the way down. Nobody uses it, but nobody's let it fall apart either, and there's the thing. Keep to it. I mean that. Everything below the line is trying to kill you, except the road. I've never known why."
"Who built it?"
"Nobody knows." Hesk shrugged, in the manner of a man who had asked and been told to stop. "It's older than the kingdom. It's older than the wall, and the wall is four hundred years old."
He held out his hand. After a moment Kael understood and shook it. The corporal's grip was dry and brief and slightly apologetic.
"If you find anyone down there," Hesk said, "tell them the Marches aren't a wall. They're a sort of apology."
Kael did not ask what he meant. He suspected the corporal didn't know either, only that it was a thing a soldier thought and had never said aloud. The cart turned. The mule gave Kael a parting look of pure contempt. Within a quarter of an hour the sound of wheels had gone, and there was only wind, and the wall, and the long road falling away into the afternoon.
He stood there longer than he meant to.
The anger arrived while he was standing there, late, like a parcel that had been misrouted. It had not come in the chamber, where there had been too many people and too little air. Now there was only wind, and it had room to unpack. He had been measured by a rock, judged by a man in a clean robe, and walked four days to a cliff, and nobody in the whole process had asked him a single question he could have answered. He let it sit for the length of a long breath. Then he put it somewhere he could find it again, because it was going to be useful, and he did not have the luxury of being useless.
Leon, he thought. Not with anger, exactly. More the way you think of a coat you left on a train: someone should check it was all right. He hoped they were treating him well. He hoped the old man's smile had stayed warm.
He took a breath, shifted the bundle on his back, and walked toward it.
