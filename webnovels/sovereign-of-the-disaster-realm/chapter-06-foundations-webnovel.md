# Chapter 6: Foundations

The first thing Kael did that morning was put his hand on the gate.

He had not decided to. He had woken against the planks with a crick in his neck and ash in his teeth, and by the time he understood the yard was silent, his palm was already flat on the oak, feeling for movement in the pin.

There was none. The leaf sat in its frame with the patient weight of a thing that knew its job.

He took his hand away and found Lyra watching him from the beacon stair, one eyebrow raised a hair.

"Checking the hinge," he said.

"Mm."

"It's good practice."

"I didn't say a word."

They pushed the gate open together. It swung out heavy and slow on the pivot, and the morning came in cold and white, mist lying in the funnel of the slope, the cords of the snare line standing out of it like the ribs of a drowned boat.

The hounds were gone. He had expected that.

He had not expected the ground.

Around the base of the wall, three paces out, ran a trench. Shallow — no deeper than a hand — and it ran in long parallel grooves. Five of them, evenly spaced, curving in an unbroken line around the curve of the yard and out of sight beyond the cliff.

The earth between the grooves had been turned over like a ploughed field.

Not a track, he thought. A mark. Something had walked all the way around them in the dark, slowly, and dragged its claws behind it.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"They left before dawn," Lyra said at last. "All at once. Same as the first morning."

"What does that?" He nodded at the grooves.

"Not now."

"Lyra."

"I said, not now." She turned away from the trench and walked back into the yard without looking at it again.

He understood that this was not a refusal. It was a woman putting something on a high shelf because she needed both hands for the day.

He looked at the grooves a moment longer, then followed her in.

They had eight days of dried meat, one saw, and a collapsed roof. Kael wanted all three of those to look better in daylight. They did not.

He walked the yard slowly — the way he walked an unfamiliar flat before quoting a price. Not looking at things. Looking at what the things were doing.

The yard sloped. The long building sat tucked against the cliff with a gap in its roof you could have driven a cart through. The well in the middle had an intact stone curb and no windlass, and he had dropped a pebble into it the evening before and counted to four before the splash. That was good. A well that deep had been dug to outlast a drought.

He crouched at the centre of the yard and drew in the dirt with the screwdriver.

"Here," he said. "Gate. Well in the middle, since that's where it already is. Workshop by the gate, so anything coming in or out passes it. Stores up at the south end where—"

"No."

"I haven't finished."

"You've finished." Lyra crouched on the other side of the line he had drawn, her bad leg stuck out like a drawbridge. "Not the south end. When it rains, the cliff drains into the south end. You've seen the stain on the wall? That's the waterline. Last spring it went to my knee."

She jabbed a finger at the far corner. "That's where you want it. North, up against the rock, where the ground rises."

"That's the lookout side."

"It's also the only dry side. You put a storehouse where the cliff weeps, and you've fed the damp." She drew a rough line through his, then a second, with the blunt end of an arrow. "And not the workshop by the gate. Wind comes straight through that opening every evening. You'll lose every shaving you cut."

He looked at the dirt, and at the stain on the wall — the long black line of old water he had mistaken for a shadow.

"You've been watching this yard for a long time," he said.

"I've been drowning in it for a long time." A beat. "We can't put anything near our water problem."

She did not hear herself.

She kept drawing, scratching a new square against the rock, muttering something about the angle of the roof. Kael bent his head and said nothing at all. It was a fragile thing, that word. If he looked at it directly, it would go.

He moved the storehouse north.

The beams were the problem.

There were enough for one job. The argument over which one lasted the better part of an hour.

They stood in the long building, both looking up at the hole.

"If it rains, the meat is gone," Kael said.

"If the stair falls, I can't reach the fire."

"You've been reaching it on a rope."

"On a rope, with a knee that doesn't bend." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which was how he knew it cost her. "The stair is how I tend the beacon. If it goes, the beacon goes."

"The beacon will go out for a night, then. The meat will go rotten for good."

"You're choosing the meat over the fire."

"I'm choosing tomorrow's dinner."

For a long moment she said nothing. Her jaw had that locked look he was starting to recognise — the look of a person doing arithmetic with something she did not want to count.

"You're right," she said. It sounded like swallowing a stone.

"I'm not happy about it either."

"I know." She almost smiled. "That's the annoying thing."

They worked through the afternoon.

The roof beams came from the broken upper floor of the tower, hauled down on a sledge Kael built out of the old gate timbers. He spent more time than he meant to leaning on the wall and looking at how the pieces fit. It was a good roof. Whoever laid it had used pegs, no nails, and every rafter had a notch to take the next, so the whole thing locked up like a puzzle.

His hands knew the sequence again. The same quiet knowledge from the gate. This time he did not argue with it.

The cloud came in from the east around dusk. By the time the first drops hit the new boards, the store roof was sealed.

They stood in the doorway, shoulder to shoulder, and watched the water run off the eaves in neat grey strings, and the dried meat stayed dry.

"Huh," Lyra said.

"Yeah."

It was the best word either of them had said all day.

Kael stood in the doorway longer than the job needed.

He had been pulled across a world and walked off the edge of a map, and he could not say when he had last stood somewhere and thought of it as a thing he was responsible for.

The gate was behind him. He had not looked at it once since the rain began, and he knew exactly where it was, and what it was doing, and that it was fine.

He realised, with a small shock, that it was the first thing in this world he had decided was his.

The cost arrived that night.

He was in the long building when he heard the cry. Not a scream. Something small and surprised, cut off.

He was out of the door and across the yard before he knew he was running, and found her at the foot of the tower hanging by one hand from the rope ladder. The rung under her foot had snapped. Her bad leg dangled uselessly. Above her, the beacon pit burned in the rain, and she was still, even then, trying to get back up to it.

"Let go," Kael said.

"The fire..."

"I'll light it. Let go."

He caught her as she dropped, took the full weight of her on his shoulder, and went down on one knee in the mud. She was lighter than he expected.

She swore at him in a language he did not know, for at least a full breath. Then she stopped, and put her forehead against his collarbone and breathed.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say anything about the stair."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was going to say it's your fire, and I'll carry the wood up tonight. And tomorrow I'm going to start on the stair. That's all."

She did not answer. But she did not move away either, for a while.

He climbed the rope at dawn, because she could not, and because he wanted to see what she had been seeing for years.

The tower top was a ragged crown of stone. The beacon pit sat at the centre — a ring of black blocks about the size of a bathtub — and the fire was down to embers, which he fed with split ash from the stack until it caught.

Then he stood and looked out.

He had not realised how high they were.

The whole valley lay under him, silver-grey in the early light. The long thread of the Old Road winding up through it toward the Marches. The dead fields. The black dot of the farmstead where he had spent his first night.

And along the valley's eastern edge, dotted at long intervals on every spur of rock, he saw them.

Stumps.

Stone stubs, no higher than a house, each one capped with the same black ring. He counted five. There might have been more behind the haze.

They ran in a long line from the tower southward — each one just within sight of the next — down to the farthest edge of the valley where the land dipped and the light failed.

Not a lone tower, Kael thought.

A watch line. One post in a chain.

Somebody had built these to talk to each other. Somebody had lit them, one after another, down the whole length of the valley, so word could pass from the Marches to wherever the line ended in a single night.

"I thought they were rocks," said a voice at his shoulder.

Lyra had climbed the rope — which she must not have been able to do — and stood at the lip of the pit with her face grey with pain. She was looking at the stumps the way a person looks at a crowd of strangers who might turn out to know her name.

"I never went past the first," she said. "I never had a reason."

"Lyra, you shouldn't be—"

"Look at this."

She knelt, slowly, and with both hands tipped a flat slab up from the rim of the pit. He had taken it for part of the wall.

On its underside, where no weather had ever reached, was the star.

Seven points, cut deep and perfectly clean, so sharp it might have been carved last week. The embers lit it from below, and it glowed a little in the dim.

One of the points was longer than the others.

A groove ran out of its tip — straight as a ruled line — across the lip of the pit and into the stone beyond.

He followed it with his eyes. When she let the slab down into its bed, the groove lined up with a notch in the rim and ran out over the edge of the tower into the dawn.

It ran down the valley, toward the stumps.

Toward the last one, he thought — or the one before it. The haze lay thick at the far end, and a groove cut in stone was a thinner thing than a line of sight. He shifted his head an inch and the answer changed. He shifted it back and it changed again.

"I never knew," Lyra said, low. "All these years looking at this, and I never traced the line."

Kael said nothing. He knelt beside her in the ash, and the fire popped, and neither of them was thinking, now, about tonight.

"We're going to need a bigger plan," Lyra said.

She had said we again. This time, he saw, she heard it.

"Two people," Kael said.

Lyra looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded — once, small and sharp, like a nail going in true.

"Two people," she said.

And below them, down the line of dark stumps, the morning wind moved through the dead fields like a hand passing over the page of a book.
